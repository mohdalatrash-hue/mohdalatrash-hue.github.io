Reading & Meaning: Scientific Terms in Context

Read the passage first, then work out what the terms mean — from Greek and Latin word parts, and above all from the words around them.

🔍 Why this matters

No one memorises every scientific term. Skilled readers work them out — by breaking the word into parts they already know, and by reading the sentence around it for a definition, a contrast or an example. This workshop trains both moves on four kinds of scientific writing. You do not need any science background.

🧩 Three tools, used in this order. (1) Break the word into parts you recognise. (2) Read the context for a cue that defines or limits it. (3) Ask whether this is an ordinary English word being used in a special technical sense — the commonest trap of all.
Tool 1 · Word parts

A caution: word parts give you a direction, not a definition. Antibiotic is literally “against life,” but it means a drug that kills bacteria — not a poison. Always confirm the guess against the sentence.
Tool 2 · Context cues

Scientific writers expect readers who do not know every term, so they build help into the sentence. Four cues do most of the work:

  • Definition cue — the meaning is stated outright: “Latency, that is, the delay between a request and its response, rose sharply.” Watch for that is, in other words, is defined as, refers to, or.
  • Apposition — a gloss set off by commas, dashes or brackets: “the myocardium (the muscular wall of the heart) was undamaged.”
  • Contrast cue — the opposite is named: “The tumour was benign, not malignant.” Watch for unlike, whereas, in contrast, rather than, not.
  • Example cue — instances are listed: “Volatile compounds — ethanol, acetone and ammonia — evaporate at room temperature.” Watch for such as, including, for example, e.g.

Passage 1 · Medicine
A man of fifty-eight was referred to the cardiology clinic after a routine check showed sustained hypertension — a resting blood pressure persistently above the accepted threshold. He reported no chest pain. An initial scan found a small benign growth on the chest wall: it was well defined, slow-growing, and showed no sign of invading the surrounding tissue, unlike a malignant tumour.

The team’s first concern, however, was the heart muscle itself. Blood tests showed a raised level of troponin, a protein released when myocardial cells are injured, indicating that a small region of heart muscle had been deprived of oxygen.

The etiology of the raised blood pressure was not obvious. No single cause could be identified, and the team recorded it as multifactorial, arising from diet, long inactivity and a family history of the same condition. One drug that would normally be offered was contraindicated in this patient, because his kidney function was already impaired and the drug would have worsened it. A different agent was chosen instead — one whose action on heart-muscle cells had first been characterised in vitro, in cultured cells outside the body, before any trial in patients. With treatment and weight loss the prognosis was described as good. Adapted from a clinical overview written for ENG 203 · not a real patient record

Passage 2 · Computer Science
When the team measured the search service, the dominant complaint was latency — the delay between a user pressing Enter and the first result appearing on screen. For most requests the delay was about forty milliseconds, but the slowest one per cent took almost two seconds. These slow requests were not doing more work; they were waiting. Each triggered a fresh lookup in the main database, because the cache, a small fast store holding copies of recently requested results, had been sized too small to hold a working day’s queries.

Two changes followed. The first enlarged the cache. The second increased concurrency, so that several independent lookups could be in progress over the same period instead of strictly one after another. This is not free: each additional task carries overhead — the extra memory and coordination that the mechanism itself consumes and that does no useful work for the user.

One recursive function, which had been written in terms of itself, was rewritten as a simple loop. The old ranking interface was marked deprecated: it still works and existing code still calls it, but new code must not use it and it will be removed in a later release. The ranking step was kept deterministic: the same query on the same index always returns the same ordering. Adapted from an engineering design note written for ENG 203
⚛️ Read first, then answer. Physics is strict about pairs of words that ordinary English treats as synonyms — speed and velocity, mass and weight, heat and temperature. The distinction is usually stated in the sentence itself.
Passage 3 · Physics
A steel trolley is released down a gentle ramp and its motion is recorded. Students must report the trolley’s velocity and not merely its speed: velocity states both how fast the trolley moves and the direction in which it moves, whereas speed states the magnitude alone. Friction between wheels and ramp opposes the motion, and its strength is summarised by a coefficient — a pure number, carrying no units, that characterises the particular pair of surfaces in contact.

The second experiment uses a mass hanging from a spring. Once the mass is set oscillating, the amplitude is measured: the greatest distance the mass reaches from its rest position. The same term is used of travelling waves, where it is paired with wavelength, the distance from one crest to the next. Left alone, the oscillation dies away and the mass settles where the spring’s upward pull exactly balances its weight. There the system is in equilibrium, since the forces on the mass sum to zero.

Two further ideas appear in outline. A body at rest resists being set moving, and a moving one resists being stopped; that resistance is inertia, and mass is its measure. The energy lost to friction and to the air is never recovered in usable form: the entropy of the laboratory has increased. Adapted from an introductory laboratory handout written for ENG 203
🔬 Read first, then answer. One setting, three sciences: biology, chemistry and earth science share a vocabulary. Notice how often the writer supplies a definition immediately after the term, set off by a dash or a comma.
Passage 4 · General Sciences
A shallow lake is a convenient meeting point for several sciences. At the surface, algae carry out photosynthesis: using energy from sunlight they combine carbon dioxide and water into sugars, releasing oxygen as a by-product. Which algae are present is a question of taxonomy, the orderly classification of living things into named groups such as genus and species.

Below the surface, chemistry takes over. The solubility of oxygen in water — that is, the greatest amount that will dissolve at a given temperature — falls as the water warms, so a hot summer leaves less oxygen available to fish. Dissolved minerals behave differently again. When calcium and carbonate ions meet in sufficient concentration they form solid calcium carbonate, which is almost insoluble and leaves the solution as a precipitate. Grain by grain that material settles to the bottom and joins the sediment, the layer of particles that has settled out of the water over time.

Living things in the lake must hold their internal conditions steady while the water around them changes; this regulation is called homeostasis. Enzymes make it possible. An enzyme is a biological catalyst: a substance that speeds up a chemical reaction without itself being used up, so a very small quantity goes on working indefinitely. Adapted from a general-science reading written for ENG 203
