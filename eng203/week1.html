What scientific terminology is — and why an English major studies it

Scientific terminology is the vocabulary the sciences use to name things precisely: parts of the body, states of matter, processes, instruments, diseases, operations. What makes it worth a whole course is that it is systematic. Ordinary English vocabulary has to be learned word by word; scientific vocabulary is largely assembled from a limited stock of Greek and Latin pieces that behave the same way every time. Gastritis is gastr- (stomach) + -itis (inflammation). Once you know -itis, you also know arthritis, dermatitis, hepatitis and a hundred others you have never seen. For a student of English this matters twice over. First, it is a large and practical slice of the lexicon — useful for translation, for teaching, for medical or technical work, for reading anything scientific in English. Second, it is a live demonstration of how English morphology actually works: borrowing, compounding, affixation and combining forms, all visible in a single vocabulary. You are learning words, and you are learning how words are made.

A first look at roots, prefixes and suffixes

This is the spine of the whole course. Nothing to memorise yet — just meet the three kinds of piece and see how they combine.

The root

Carries the core meaning — usually the thing the term is about. cardi- heart, derm- skin, therm- heat, photo- light, crypt- hidden.

The prefix

Goes in front and modifies — direction, position, degree, number, negation. hyper- above/excessive, hypo- below/deficient, sub- under, micro- small, a-/an- without.

The suffix

Goes at the end and tells you what kind of term it is — a condition, a procedure, a specialist, a study. -itis inflammation, -ology the study of, -ectomy surgical removal, -graph a recording instrument.

Put together

hypo + therm + ia → hypothermia, a condition of below-normal heat. electro + cardio + gram → electrocardiogram, an electrical record of the heart. Same pieces, endlessly recombined.
