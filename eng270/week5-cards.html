Fiction: Two Cultures, One Story

A card-by-card speaking guide. Each card tells you what the idea means, why it matters, what to say, and what question to ask students.

Reading card 1 · Set the frame

What students are reading

Lahlali & Abu Hatab (2014), Lesson 7: “Fiction,” pp. 24–26. Tuesday 22 September 2026. This week has one class meeting only.

Say: “Today we move from general translation methods to one genre. The question is no longer only ‘Is the meaning accurate?’ It is also ‘Does this still work as fiction?’”
Open with: What can be lost from a story even when every dictionary meaning is correct?
Reading card 2 · Definition

Fiction is a genre characterized by narration

Fiction presents imagined characters and events through narration. In this lesson it normally means prose: the novel, short story, and novella.

Novel · روايةExtended fictional prose with room for developed plots and characters.
Short story · قصة قصيرةCompressed fiction, usually focused on fewer events or characters.
Novella · نوفيلا / رواية قصيرةLonger than a short story but more concentrated than a novel.
Narration · سردThe telling and ordering of events through a narrator.
Say: “Narration is not just a list of events. It is events presented through a particular voice and sequence.”
Ask: If we preserve events but flatten the narrator’s voice, have we preserved the fiction?
Reading card 3 · Sub-genres

Different kinds of fiction sound different

The reading lists mystery, historical fiction, romance, fantasy, western, science fiction, adventure, humour, medical fiction, tragedy, thriller, and horror.

Each sub-genre has linguistic expectations: a thriller may demand pace and suspense; historical fiction may use older vocabulary; humour may depend on timing or wordplay.

Example: Translating “He opened the door” in a comic scene and a horror scene may require the same event but different surrounding rhythm, description, and tone.
Ask: Which language features would you expect in horror? Which in romance?
Reading card 4 · Central difficulty

Literary translation crosses two cultures

The translator transfers more than linguistic meaning. Images, social behavior, expectations, humour, politeness, food, place, and relationships may depend on the source culture.

Say: “A dictionary connects words. A literary translator must connect readers to a cultural world.”
Source culture · ثقافة المصدرThe assumptions and world surrounding the original story.
Target culture · ثقافة الهدفThe assumptions and reading habits of the new audience.
Ask: What should a translator do when understanding a scene requires cultural knowledge the Arabic reader may not have?
Reading card 5 · Translator’s role

The translator as social mediator

A social mediator stands between two cultures and makes deliberate decisions about access, explanation, preservation, and change.

Say: “The translator does not disappear. Every decision about an unfamiliar image or idiom controls what the target reader can see.”
Concepts and imagesThe same concept may be represented differently across cultures.
Idiomatic expressionsAn idiom may carry extra emotional, cultural, or genre meaning.
Important: Mediation does not mean freely rewriting everything. A good mediator can also decide that foreignness should remain.
Ask: When does explanation help the reader, and when does it interrupt the story?
Reading card 6 · Language-family distance

English and Arabic organize meaning differently

Arabic is a Semitic language; English is Indo-European. The reading identifies syntactic, phonological, lexical, and cultural differences.

DifferencePlain meaningFictional consequence
SyntacticSentence structure and word orderEnglish narration may sound unnatural if copied.
PhonologicalSound, rhythm, rhymeNames, wordplay, and musical prose may lose effect.
LexicalWords and word combinationsNo single Arabic word may carry the same associations.
CulturalShared knowledge and practicesA scene may be linguistically clear but culturally unclear.
Ask: Which difference is most visible in dialogue? Which is most visible in descriptive prose?
Reading card 7 · Standing decision

Source-oriented or target-oriented?

Source-oriented · نحو النص المصدرKeeps more foreign names, images, structures, cultural details, and strangeness.
Target-oriented · نحو القارئ الهدفUses more familiar Arabic expressions and conventions to improve access and naturalness.

Orientation is a spectrum, not two sealed boxes. A translation can generally lean one way while making local exceptions.

Say: “The right question is not ‘Which orientation is always best?’ It is ‘What does this text, audience, and purpose require?’”
Ask: Would your decision change for an academic edition, a children’s edition, and a television adaptation?
Reading card 8 · Trade-offs

Every orientation gains something and risks something

Possible gainPossible risk
Source-orientedForeign setting, authorial texture, cultural specificityDifficulty, stiffness, or reader distance
Target-orientedFluency, immediacy, emotional accessibilityFlattening difference or changing the fictional world
Class formula: “I choose ___ because the purpose and audience require ___. I preserve ___. I accept the possible loss of ___.”
Ask: What evidence would prove that a target-oriented choice has gone too far?
Reading card 9 · Adaptation example

What the Hemingway exercise demonstrates

The textbook pairs the opening situation of The Old Man and the Sea with an Arabic rendering and asks students to find adaptation strategies.

Added story formulaThe Arabic opens with كان يا ما كان في قديم الزمان, which is not in the English. It makes the narration sound like a familiar traditional tale.
Generalized placeThe specific “Gulf Stream” becomes a general sea setting, reducing geographical specificity.
Explained/softened termThe culture-specific word salao becomes a plain statement that the man has little luck.
Compressed detailsSome emphatic phrasing and boat details are simplified.

These are observations, not automatic improvements. Ask what each change gains and what it removes.

Ask: Does the added folktale opening change the genre or only help the Arabic reader enter the story?
Reading card 10 · Exercise 7.2

Choose a method—and justify it

The Orwell exercise does not prescribe one method. Students must choose the most appropriate method for a descriptive fictional paragraph and explain why.

Teach the decision: identify purpose → identify audience → locate the difficult features → choose a method → state gains and losses → translate consistently.
Ask: Which details build the fictional world and therefore must not be casually removed?
Reading card 11 · Exercise 7.3

Communicative translation of descriptive narration

The Shirley Jackson exercise explicitly requires communicative translation. The students should make the village scene clear, fluent, and natural in Arabic while preserving time, place, atmosphere, and factual sequence.

Say: “Communicative does not mean summary. All essential facts remain; their expression becomes natural for the target reader.”
Ask: Which English sentence structures should be divided or reordered for readable Arabic?
Reading card 12 · Final teaching model

A repeatable six-question checklist

  1. What kind of fiction is this?
  2. Who is narrating, and what tone do we hear?
  3. Which details build the fictional world?
  4. Where are the linguistic or cultural gaps?
  5. Which orientation fits the purpose and readers?
  6. What does the solution gain, preserve, and risk?

One-sentence takeaway: Translating fiction means recreating narrated experience for new readers without accidentally erasing the voice and culture that make it a particular story.
