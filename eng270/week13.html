Reading check
Why does the lesson say modality ‘does the work’ in a regulation — what changes if ‘shall’ is mistranslated as ‘may’?
‘Shall’ encodes obligation while ‘may’ encodes discretion; swapping one for the other changes what the rule actually requires versus merely permits, altering the legal effect of the sentence, not just its tone.
What kind of terminology does Lesson 30 warn is easy to mistranslate in education-regulation texts?
Terminology specific to a particular school of thought (notably Islamic education) and institutional titles that do not map cleanly onto another country’s education system.
Besides vocabulary, what structural features of a regulation document does the Module 6 review ask you to carry across intact?
Numbering, cross-references, and defined terms — an administrative or legal document’s structure often carries as much meaning as its wording, so these must transfer along with the text.
What is being consolidated in this review before the course moves to legal texts proper in Weeks 14–15?
The whole administrative-texts module: register, terminological consistency, modality (obligation versus permission versus prohibition), and culture-specific institutional terminology.

Core ideas

Guidelines and regulations

Translating guidelines and regulations can be very technical, since it may involve specific terminologies and phrases. Such texts have a very limited style and specific meanings, and both should be carefully handled when translating into another language.

‘Limited style’ is a constraint on you, not a description of the author. You do not get to improve the prose. If the source repeats a defined term nine times, the target repeats it nine times — the Week 6 point about Arabic favouring lexical repetition works in your favour here.

Consistency is a quality criterion, not a preference

In a regulation, the same concept must be named by the same word every time it appears, because readers — and, eventually, adjudicators — will infer a distinction from any variation. The elegant variation that would be rewarded in a literary translation is a defect here.

Practical habit: before translating a long regulation, extract its defined terms into a list, fix an Arabic equivalent for each, and translate from that list. This is the same two-level analysis you learned for scientific texts in Week 9, applied to a different genre.

Structure carries meaning

Articles, paragraph numbering and cross-references are part of the content of a regulation, not its packaging. An article that says ‘in accordance with the provisions of Article 81, paragraph 1’ is unusable if the numbering shifts in translation.

Keep the skeleton identical: same article numbers, same paragraph divisions, same lettering. Where the target language requires a different order within a sentence, the sentence may be reordered; the document may not.

Modality — the sentence that does the work

Administrative and regulatory texts are built out of obligation (shall, must), permission (may), and prohibition (shall not, may not). English legal drafting uses shall for obligation in a way ordinary English no longer does, and translating it as a simple future is a substantive error, not a stylistic one.

Read every modal twice and ask what the sentence does: does it impose a duty, grant a discretion, or forbid something? Then choose the Arabic construction that does the same. This is the bridge into Weeks 14–15, where the directive function of legal texts is treated head on.

Lesson 30 · Learning and teaching regulations

What this sub-genre demands

Translating learning and teaching regulations is not a straightforward process: it requires substantial knowledge of the field of student education. Familiarity with the register is what makes an accurate and reliable translation possible — and this is a register you can study in advance, since every university publishes one.

The style, as elsewhere in this module, is very limited in its use of rhetorical devices. That is a constraint on the translator: there is nothing to interpret and everything to get exactly right.

Culture-specific institutional terminology

The textbook singles out one further challenge: registers pertaining to Islamic education, where terminology may be very specific and associated with a particular school of thought or religious sect. The translator has to carry such culture-specific terms into the target language without distorting the meaning of the source.

The remedy is awareness of the equivalents in the target language — which means knowing not only what a term means but whose usage it belongs to. This is the Week 7 cultural problem again, arriving inside an administrative document.

The same care applies to institutional titles generally. A post such as Graduate Teaching Assistant names a position inside one system; a literal calque can name a post that does not exist in the target institution and confer duties that do not apply. Match the role where an equivalent exists, and describe it where none does.

Check yourself
Your source regulation uses the competent authority eleven times. Should your Arabic vary the phrasing for readability?
No. In a regulation the same concept must carry the same name every time, because any variation invites the inference that a different thing is meant. Lexical repetition is also normal Arabic cohesion, so nothing is lost.
Why is translating shall as a future tense a substantive error?
Because in this register shall imposes an obligation. Rendering it as a prediction turns a duty into a forecast and changes what the document does — the text’s directive function is its content.
What must be preserved exactly, even where the sentence has to be reordered in Arabic?
The document skeleton: article numbers, paragraph divisions, lettering and cross-references. A provision that refers to ‘Article 81, paragraph 1’ is only usable if that reference still points at the same text.
How does the Week 9 method transfer to this genre?
Through the two-level analysis. Lexical analysis becomes a defined-terms list with fixed Arabic equivalents; grammatical analysis becomes attention to modality and to the structural contrasts between the two languages. The genre changes; the procedure does not.
