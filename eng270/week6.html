Core ideas

Narrative, and what the translator has to see

Narration is the most distinctive feature of fiction. It forms the plot of the story or novel, which has a specific setting and a point of view — the voice of the narrator. The translator’s awareness of the elements of fiction and of narrative technique is what makes the process manageable.

Technically, a narrative text contains three layers — the text, the story and the fabula — and deals with a series of connected events caused or experienced by the actors (Bal 1985). You are not asked to become a narratologist; you are asked to notice that a narrative has a voice and a viewpoint, and that both can be destroyed in translation without a single word being mistranslated.

Discourse problems — cohesion and coherence

Discourse problems are problems of cohesion and coherence, and English and Arabic build them differently.

  • Arabic relies heavily on conjunction as a cohesive device; English uses more subordination.
  • Run-on sentences are usual in Arabic, since punctuation is not traditionally used as a cohesive device.
  • English uses subordinates and general words as lexical cohesive devices; Arabic favours repetition of the same lexical item (Dickins 2002, 2009).

In a comprehensive taxonomy, English cohesive devices classify as reference, substitution, ellipsis, conjunction and lexical cohesion. The practical consequence: an English paragraph of long subordinated sentences often becomes an Arabic paragraph of coordinated clauses — and repeating the noun where English would have used a pronoun or a general word is good Arabic, not clumsy Arabic.

Syntactic problems — word order

English is usually considered an SVO language; Arabic is basically VSO, since SVO structures are less frequent. A translator working into Arabic must take that difference into account rather than importing English order.

English also makes heavy use of auxiliary verbs in questions and negation. Such use is not frequent in Arabic, so auxiliaries are not always translated.

Tense and aspect — the real difficulty

Arabic tense is classified into past, present and future, and depends on inflections of both verb and subject. One verb form covers several categories of past or present, and adverbs are what disambiguate.

Three rules cover most cases:

  • The perfective aspect is normally indicated by adding an adverb: توًا، حالاً، في التو، على الفور، لتوهم.
  • The past perfect uses the construction كان + قد and its derivatives.
  • The progressive is not carried by auxiliaries in Standard Arabic; it too depends on adverbs, combined with كان and its derivatives for the past progressive.

Check yourself
Your Arabic paragraph repeats the same noun four times where the English used it, the matter and this issue. Is that a fault?
No — it is normal Arabic cohesion. English uses subordinates and general words as lexical cohesive devices; Arabic favours repetition of the same lexical item. Forcing English-style variation into Arabic is the more common error.
Translate the aspectual difference: They have just arrived versus They arrived yesterday.
The Arabic verb is the same in both. The difference is carried by the adverb: توًا / حالاً for the perfective, أمس for the simple past. Adverbs, not tenses, do the aspectual work in Arabic.
An English source has one 60-word sentence with three subordinate clauses. What is likely to happen in Arabic?
It becomes a run of coordinated clauses joined by conjunction — which is normal, because Arabic relies on conjunction where English relies on subordination, and run-on sentences are usual since punctuation is not traditionally a cohesive device in Arabic.
Why are English auxiliary verbs often left untranslated?
Because English uses auxiliaries heavily in questions and negation, and such use is not frequent in Arabic. Translating them mechanically produces the يكون / يملك problem you met in Week 2.
