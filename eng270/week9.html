Reading check
Give the textbook’s example of Arabic grammatical gender in scientific vocabulary that English does not have.
The atom is grammatically feminine in Arabic and the proton masculine, with the verb required to agree accordingly — a distinction English speakers must learn deliberately, since English nouns for objects carry no gender.
Name two of Baker’s word-level equivalence problems.
Any two of: no TL equivalent for a word, no superordinate term available, and the other word-level gaps Baker catalogues.
What remedy does Baker suggest for catching these equivalence problems?
Set the draft translation aside and reread the target text alone, without the source in view, to judge whether it reads naturally and completely on its own terms.
What is the difference between cohesion and coherence?
Cohesion is the surface-level glue of a text — its connectors and explicit linking devices; coherence is its underlying narrative or logical sense. A text can remain coherent even with almost no connectors, because coherence is about sense, not surface glue-words.
Give the example the lesson uses to show a text can be coherent without much cohesion.
Dickins’s ‘kitchen’ example — a short passage that makes complete sense with minimal explicit connectors, showing coherence does not require heavy cohesive marking.
What is being consolidated in the Module 4 review (pp. 120–124)?
The whole scientific-texts module: borrowing and coinage strategies, the two-level lexical/grammatical analysis, Baker’s word-level and above-word-level equivalence problems, and the cohesion/coherence distinction.

Core ideas

Lesson 21 · Inflection, gender and number

Arabic is a highly inflected language compared with English, which uses very few inflections. Arabic inflects for tense, number and gender, and where English inflects for plural only, Arabic distinguishes singular, dual and plural.

The consequence in scientific prose is immediate. English uses it for both the electron and the atom; Arabic treats the electron as masculine and the atom as feminine, and the verbs that follow carry that distinction. A translator copying English pronoun use will produce agreement errors on every page — and agreement errors in a technical text read as incompetence.

Lesson 22 · Equivalence at word level

Problems with finding the right scientific equivalent depend on the type of word involved. Baker (1992/2006) lists the recurring cases: culture-specific concepts; a source concept not lexicalised in the TL; a semantically complex source word; different distinctions in meaning between the two languages; a missing superordinate; a missing specific term or hyponym; differences in physical or interpersonal perspective; differences in expressive meaning; differences in form; differences in frequency and purpose of using specific forms; and loan words in the source text.

Her strategies answer them: translation by a more general word (superordinate), by a more neutral or less expressive word, and by cultural substitution, among others. You have met all three before under different names — in Week 7, as Harvey’s and Graedler’s culture-bound strategies.

Lesson 23 · Equivalence above word level

These problems concern collocation, idioms and fixed expressions.

For collocations, Baker identifies: the engrossing effect of source-text patterning; misinterpreting the meaning of a source collocation; the tension between accuracy and naturalness; culture-specific collocations; and marked collocations in the source.

Her solutions are practical rather than theoretical, and worth adopting for your own assignments: take the effect of the source into consideration; put the draft aside for a few hours and return to read the target text, so that source patterning interferes less; evaluate the significance of any potential change in meaning; and translate by a marked collocation where the target language and the purpose of the translation allow it.

For idioms and fixed expressions the problems begin with recognition — you cannot solve an idiom you have read literally — and continue with: no equivalent in the TL; a similar counterpart used in a different context; an idiom used in both its literal and its idiomatic sense at once; and differences in convention, context and frequency of use.

Lesson 24 · Equivalence at text level

Cohesion and coherence are the two main aspects of discourse. Coherence is a tacit, but discernible, thematic or emotional development running through the text (Dickins 2005). His worked example is a short passage with almost no cohesive markers that is nonetheless perfectly coherent, because the events are in chronological order.

Two problems and their strategies:

  • The tension between word order and communicative function. Strategies: voice change, change of the verb, nominalisation, extraposition.
  • Maintaining a balance between accuracy and naturalness in the cohesion of the text. Strategies include gender adding, person deleting, verb tense reordering, restrictions of word order, producing different lexical chains, rechunking (reorganising or renumbering paragraphs and sentences), and repunctuating by text type (Baker 1992).

The last of these connects directly back to Week 6: Arabic conjunction against English subordination, and the fact that punctuation is not traditionally an Arabic cohesive device.

Check yourself
Your Arabic mirrors every English word pairing and reads oddly. What is the named cause, and the named remedy?
The engrossing effect of source-text patterning. Baker’s remedy is to put the draft aside for a few hours and then reread the target text on its own terms, so that the interference is reduced. Worth doing before you submit anything on this course.
English uses it for both the electron and the atom. What must your Arabic do?
Treat the electron as masculine and the atom as feminine, and inflect the following verbs accordingly. Grammatical gender is not optional in Arabic, and English pronoun use is no guide to it.
What is the first problem Baker names for idioms, and why does it come first?
Recognition. Everything else — finding an equivalent, judging context and frequency — presupposes that you noticed the expression was an idiom. An idiom read literally is not a difficult translation problem; it is an undetected one.
Give an example of a coherent text with almost no cohesive markers.
Dickins’s: I was hungry. I went downstairs. I knew the kitchen was on the ground floor… I made myself a sandwich. There are no connectives, yet the chronological narrative structure makes the passage coherent. Cohesion and coherence are separate properties.
