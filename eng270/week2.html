Core ideas

Method, and the one axis everything sits on

A method of translation is the way we translate — literally or freely. Every classification you will meet this semester, however elaborate, is a refinement of that one old opposition. Ghazala splits the literal side into three; Week 3 splits the free side into two; Week 4 adds the methods Newmark contributes on top.

Keep the axis in your head. When you are stuck in a practical session, the useful question is not ‘what does this word mean?’ but ‘how far along the literal–free axis am I allowed to go with this text?’

1 · Word-for-word translation (literal translation of words)

The translation of individual words: find the Arabic equivalent for each English word, regardless of differences in grammar, word order, context or special use. The whole attention is on the source language.

It is risky for six reasons, and you should be able to list them:

  • It ignores the TL completely.
  • It disregards the grammatical differences between the two languages.
  • It transfers SL grammar and word order.
  • It has no respect for context — words are understood in isolation rather than in combination.
  • It transfers only the primary meaning of words.
  • It cannot deal with SL words that have no TL equivalent.

2 · One-to-one literal translation

Each SL word or phrase becomes an identical word or phrase in the TL — same number, same grammatical class, same type of language: a noun for a noun, an adjective for an adjective. Crucially, this is done in context.

Similar to word-for-word in two respects: it keeps the SL word order, and it insists on the same type and number of words.Different from word-for-word in two respects: it takes context into consideration, and it translates collocational, special and metaphorical items into their TL equivalents.

It is still unsatisfactory when it drags SL grammar across (translating every be as يكون, every have as يملك, every personal pronoun) or when it insists on matching the word count.

3 · Literal translation of meaning (direct translation)

This method is keen to translate the meaning as closely, accurately and completely as possible. It is the translation of meaning in context, and it respects TL grammar and word order. It can be described as the full translation of meaning — and it is the best of the three literal methods.

It also dissolves a confusion students carry in from school: a word does not have one ‘literal meaning’. It has a common or primary meaning, and then it has whatever the context requires.

Worked examples
English sourceArabic renderingWhat is happening
That child is intelligentذاك الطفل يكون ذكيًاWord-for-word. Every English word gets an Arabic counterpart, including the copula, which Arabic does not need here. The result is grammatical debris.
Let us shake handsدعنا نتصافح بالأيديOne-to-one, and unsatisfactory. بالأيدي is forced in to match the English word count. Better: دعنا نتصافح.
I have blind confidence in youأنا أملك ثقة عمياء بكOne-to-one. The metaphor survives, but have is dragged across as a verb. The direct translation of meaning drops it: عندي ثقة عمياء بك.
to run in a raceيجري / يركض في السباقLiteral translation of meaning. The primary sense fits here — but only here.
to run a companyيدير شركةSame verb, different context, different Arabic. There is no single ‘literal meaning’ of run to fall back on.
in the long runعلى المدى البعيدThe verb has disappeared entirely. Context, not the dictionary, decided that.

Check yourself
Which literal method is ‘full translation of meaning’ a description of?
Literal translation of meaning, also called direct translation — the third and best member of the literal family. Note the contrast the textbook draws: direct translation is the full translation of meaning, not of lexis or of style.
Name the two respects in which one-to-one literal translation still resembles word-for-word.
It retains the SL word order in the TL, and it insists on having the same type and number of words. Those two habits are exactly what makes it fail on shake hands.
Why can we not say that يجري is ‘the literal meaning’ of run?
Because run has no single literal meaning — it has a common or primary meaning plus whatever the context calls for. Compare run in a race, run a company, in the long run: three contexts, three unrelated Arabic renderings.
Word-for-word translation cannot handle SL words with no TL equivalent. Which later week solves that problem, and how?
Week 9, on scientific texts: borrowing (total borrowing, calque, naturalisation) and coinage (derivation, al-istinbāt, loan translation) are the named strategies for exactly this gap.
