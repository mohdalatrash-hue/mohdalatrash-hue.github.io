Reading check
Why does Lesson 7 call literary translation the hardest genre on this course?
Because the translator mediates between two cultures, not merely two languages — fiction carries cultural assumptions that a purely linguistic translation cannot transfer.
What does it mean to call the translator a ‘social mediator’, and what choice does that role force?
It means the translator stands between the source culture and the target culture and must consciously decide whether to stay source-oriented (preserving foreign flavour) or target-oriented (naturalising for the target reader).
Why is English–Arabic literary translation described as structurally demanding, beyond the usual difficulty of literary work?
English and Arabic belong to different language families, so literary devices built on English’s grammar or sound often have no direct structural equivalent in Arabic and must be recreated rather than transferred.

Core ideas

Why literary translation is the hard case

Translating literary texts is more demanding than other types because it deals with two cultures — a source culture and a target culture — and not merely two languages.

The translator therefore has the duty of a social mediator. Two decisions follow from that role and recur in every literary text you touch: how to translate concepts represented in the SL and TL using different ideas and images, and how to translate idiomatic expressions that carry extra meaning in the genre being translated.

The course treats three literary genres — fiction, drama and poetry — and for each one asks the same three questions: what are the distinctive features of the genre, what problems does it create in translation, and what strategies deal with those problems.

Fiction

Fiction names a literary genre characterised by narration, usually prose in the form of a novel, a short story or a novella. It sub-classifies into themes — mystery, historical, romance, fantasy, western, science fiction, action adventure, humour, medical, tragedy, thriller, horror, and more — and each sub-genre has its own linguistic characteristics.

Translating fiction is challenging in general because it relies so heavily on narration. Beyond that, the translator has to make a standing decision: source-language-oriented or target-language-oriented? Adapting, managing and monitoring are the further issues that follow from it.

Two language families, one text

When translating from English into Arabic the job is more demanding still, because the two languages belong to different families: Arabic is Semitic, English is Indo-European. They differ in their syntactic, phonological and lexical systems, on top of complicated cultural differences.

This is not a complaint; it is the reason the next two weeks exist. Week 6 takes the discourse and syntactic consequences of that gap; Week 7 takes the cultural ones.

In class — Tuesday

First in-class translation — a literary text

The opening literary practical is an extract from Charles Dickens’s Great Expectations — the convict seizing Pip in the churchyard. It is chosen because it is almost all dialogue, and dialogue is where the orientation decision bites first.

Work through it in class with a dictionary. Three things to watch: the threatening register of the convict’s speech, the child’s frightened politeness, and the fact that English exclamatory dialogue does not survive word-for-word treatment. Keep your draft — you will revisit it in Week 6 once you have the discourse and syntax tools.

This is class practice, not graded work. The graded in-class group task falls in Week 10.

Check yourself
Why is the literary translator described as a social mediator rather than a language converter?
Because the work sits between two cultures. Concepts, images and idiomatic expressions carry meaning that exists in the source culture and may have no counterpart in the target one; the translator has to decide how that meaning will reach a reader who does not share the source culture.
What is the standing decision a translator of fiction has to make?
Whether to be source-language-oriented or target-language-oriented — and to make that choice deliberately, for the whole text, rather than drifting between the two. Adapting, managing and monitoring follow from it.
Why does the English–Arabic pair make literary translation structurally harder?
The two languages belong to different language families — Arabic is Semitic, English is Indo-European — so their syntactic, phonological and lexical systems differ, and the cultural distance is added on top of that.
