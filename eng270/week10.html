Core ideas

What a media text is

Media texts are the main means of receiving information and entertainment: the language of articles and advertisements published in newspapers or broadcast on radio, TV and websites. They depend on the addressee, and their main function is to express the message effectively — which is why they emphasise layout and mode of presentation.

In newspapers the message is aided by heavy focus on the display of headlines and banners — font size, organisation, prominence, typography — in whatever way most appeals to readers. Being a sub-category of non-fiction, media texts do not always apply the literary use of language.

Structure — the lead, not the introduction

Careful structuring matters for every text, but media texts open differently. Where a literary text habitually offers a clear introduction, a media text opens with what is called a lead, used to create suspense or surprise, or to shock or arouse emotion in the reader (Rowland and Avery 2001).

Structuring is vital not only for strengthening an argument but for the coherent flow of information — and in media writing the flow is front-loaded.

Style — short, plain and active

Media language is freer than standard language and is geared to producing news within a very limited timeframe. The consequences are concrete:

  • Sentences and paragraphs are short and economical.
  • Active verbs are preferred over passive, and adjectives are limited.
  • Elaborate cohesive devices are less common; because media text aims to grab attention, emotive language is used more.
  • In Arabic, media sentences are less complex, conveying information away from creative stylistic aspects; media texts emphasise plain language.

Media language is less elaborate than other types. That is a specification of the target register, not a licence to write badly.

Headlines

When media language is attention-grabbing it tends to be free from grammatical constraints. A newspaper headline reads ‘State population to double by 2040; babies to blame’ rather than the fully grammatical ‘The state population is expected to double by 2040 and babies are the ones to blame’. Function words are omitted and only the meaningful lexis is kept.

The headline has a range of functions that specifically dictate its shape, content and structure, and operates within restrictions that limit the writer’s freedom (Reah 2002). For the translator this means a headline is translated as a headline — you reconstruct the function in Arabic, you do not expand the ellipsis into a sentence.

The four categories

Media texts divide into hard news, feature articles, special-topic news and headlines, each with different features depending on theme and structure.

Different media also have their own characteristics: magazine covers carry less text and no advertising compared with newspapers, while a newspaper runs separate sections — sports, entertainment, TV guide, medical and scientific news — each with its own genre-specific language. Some texts are written very quickly with the sole aim of publishing on the same day (Bell 1991), and may originate as an interview, a press release, a government briefing, or agency copy — each differing in form and language.

Check yourself
What is a ‘lead’, and how does it differ from an introduction?
A lead is the opening of a media text, used to create suspense or surprise, or to shock or arouse emotion. A literary text offers a clear introduction that sets up what follows; a media lead front-loads the most arresting material instead.
Translate the function, not the form: ‘State population to double by 2040; babies to blame’. What must survive into Arabic?
Its headline character: brevity, the omission of function words, the punch of the second clause. Expanding it into a full grammatical Arabic sentence would be an accurate translation of the words and a failed translation of the text type.
Name the four categories of media text.
Hard news, feature articles, special-topic news, and headlines.
Why do media texts favour active verbs and few adjectives?
Because the genre is produced under time pressure for rapid comprehension. Active structures and lean modification carry information faster — and Arabic already favours active structures, so this is one of the rare places where the two languages pull the same way.
