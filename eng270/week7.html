Core ideas

Culture is the second half of the job

Translation is not simply a matter of translating words. When you translate you are working with language and culture — language being one of the many social activities through which the culture of its speakers is manifested. You stand as a mediator between SL and TL, and you need to understand the culture of the audience you are communicating with.

Cultural problems are also sociolinguistic: politeness and terms of address, and discourse-level matters such as speech acts, maxims of speech, implicature and inference. Each culture manifests these differently, and the translator has to know how.

Graedler’s four steps for culturally bound words

Four moves are available when a word carries culture rather than only sense (Graedler 2000: 3). The textbook illustrates all four on the same Saudi pastry, كليجا:

  • Making up a new word — datebite.
  • Explaining the meaning of the SL expression instead of translating it — date-filled spice pastry.
  • Preserving the SL term — Kleija.
  • Opting for a TL word that seems similar or has the same relevance — cookie, pastry, biscuit, filled pastry.

Notice that the four are ordered by how much of the source culture survives. That ordering is the decision you are making.

Harvey’s four strategies

A second, overlapping set (Harvey 2000: 2–6):

  • Functional equivalence — use a TL referent whose function is similar to the SL one: bar → café.
  • Formal equivalence — linguistic or word-for-word equivalence: fast food وجبات سريعة.
  • Transcription or borrowing — reproduce or transliterate the original term; the far end of SL-oriented strategies: telephone تلفون.
  • Descriptive or self-explanatory translation — generic terms that convey the meaning: Lexus → luxury car; كليجا → a traditional Saudi date-filled spiced pastry.

Domestication and foreignisation

Two further strategies bridge cultural gaps (Venuti 1995: 49). Domestication makes the translated text closer to the TL culture, so that the foreign text is imprinted with values specific to the target culture. Foreignisation preserves the values of the source text, resisting dominant target-language cultural values in order to signify the linguistic and cultural difference of the foreign text.

The argument is live, and you are expected to be able to state both sides. Supporters of domestication say it helps the reader understand the text easily. Critics say it violates the source culture and discourages the critical thought that comes from being challenged by difference.

Drama, and why adaptation dominates it

Drama differs from fiction in one decisive respect: it is written to be performed on stage. Dialogue therefore carries most of the weight, and the translator deals with meta-linguistic elements too — body language and gesture. Prosodic features demand special attention, since stage dialogue is characterised by rhythm, intonation, pitch and loudness, none of which is obvious from reading the script in isolation.

Adaptation and domestication are consequently the most frequent techniques in drama translation. Adaptation is a set of translative interventions producing a text not generally accepted as a translation but nevertheless recognised as representing a source text. Its procedures (Bastin 2009) are: transcription, omission, expansion, exoticism, updating, situational or cultural adequacy, and creation. It may be local (parts of the text, internal causes) or global (the whole text, external causes).
Poetry — the hardest genre of all

Why poetry resists translation

Poetry is thought to be the most challenging genre for the translator. What distinguishes it from the others is its reliance on figurative language, rhyme and rhythm. To be a poet is to deviate from the usual linguistic norm — to play with words and twist them — and those foregrounding strategies put pressure on a translator working between two languages as linguistically and culturally distant as Arabic and English.

Before you begin, read the source poem carefully, understand it, and read about the poet — the ideology, the recurring metaphors, the diction that can only be recognised across several poems. That reading gives you a direction; without it you are translating lines rather than a poem.

Six strategies for translating poetry (after Bassnett-McGuire)

  • Phonemic translation — recreates the sounds of the SL in the TL while transferring meaning at the same time.
  • Literal translation — word-for-word rendering.
  • Metrical translation — reproduces the original meter. Not always successful, since each language has its own metrical structure.
  • Verse-to-prose translation — produces prose from a poem. Its biggest weakness is the loss of the beauty of the original, or of the poetic shape.
  • Rhymed translation — transfers the rhyme of the original. The result is phonetically appropriate but tends to be semantically inappropriate.
  • Free verse translation — achieves accurate equivalents at the expense of literary value.

Each strategy buys one thing and sells another. The exam does not ask which is best; it asks you to name the strategy and state the trade.

Metaphor, and Newmark’s seven procedures

Poetry is a condensed form of language, rich with meaning, and it works through imagery and figures of speech — metaphor above all. Newmark (1988b) sets out seven procedures for translating a metaphor:

  • Reproducing the same image in the TL.
  • Replacing the SL image with a standard TL image.
  • Translating the metaphor as a simile.
  • Translating the metaphor or simile as a simile plus sense.
  • Converting the metaphor into sense — explanation.
  • Deletion.
  • Translating the same metaphor combined with sense.

The list runs, roughly, from most image-preserving to least. Choosing from it is the poetic equivalent of choosing along the literal–free axis.

Check yourself
Produce both a domesticated and a foreignised Arabic rendering of Their story reminds you of Romeo and Juliet, and say what each costs.
Domesticated: تذكرك قصتهم بقيس وليلى — immediately intelligible, but it erases the fact that the source text is English and the allusion Shakespearean. Foreignised: تذكرك قصتهم بروميو وجوليت — keeps the source culture visible, at the cost of assuming the reader knows the reference.
Which of Harvey’s strategies stands at the far SL-oriented end, and why?
Transcription or borrowing — reproducing or transliterating the original term. Nothing of the source item is converted, so the source culture is maximally preserved and the target reader is given the least help.
A translator renders كليجا as cookie. Which strategy is that, and what has been lost?
Graedler’s fourth step — opting for a TL word of similar relevance. It reads naturally but loses the specificity: the dates, the spices, and the Saudi setting all disappear. Descriptive translation (a traditional Saudi date-filled spiced pastry) keeps them at the cost of fluency.
Why does drama attract adaptation more than fiction does?
Because drama is written to be performed. Dialogue, prosody, gesture and audience response all have to work in the theatre of the target culture, and adaptation — preserving theme, character and plot while converting the culture and rewriting the text — is what makes that possible.
