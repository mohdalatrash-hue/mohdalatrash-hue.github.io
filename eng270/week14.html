Core ideas

Legal language

Legal language is known to be very strict and precise, with no freedom for a creative style. If you have been enjoying the range of choice available in the literary weeks, this is where it closes.

Although legal translation belongs to specialised translation, it shares some characteristics of general translation — which is why the methods of Weeks 2–4 still apply. What changes is how far along the literal–free axis you are permitted to travel: in this genre, very little.

Classifying legal texts

Legal texts can be classified according to the functions of legal discourse and in connection with the sub-types of legal discourse. Similar to other specialised texts, legal texts serve an informative function according to Reiss (cf. Šarčević 1997: 7).

They can also have directive and imperative functions, as well as an expressive one (Newmark 1982). Sager (1993) adds the point that makes the classification useful rather than academic: legal texts are informative for the general reader and directive for a specific group of people — the same document, two audiences, two functions.

The three functions in practice

  • Directive — the text guides behaviour by setting out norms, rules and standards that tell people how they ought to act. Statutes, regulations, contractual obligations. Here the modality is the content, and the Week 13 discipline applies without relaxation.
  • Informative — the text tells a reader what the law is: commentaries, explanatory notes, the recitals of a convention. Precision still governs, but the target reader is being informed rather than bound.
  • Expressive — the text carries the voice and authority of its author: judgments, preambles, declarations. This is the one function in the genre where style is doing work, and it is still not an invitation to be creative.

Identify the function of the passage in front of you before you choose anything else. A translator who renders a directive provision in the register of a commentary has mistranslated the document even if every word is right.

Where the earlier weeks come back

Almost everything you have learned reappears here in a stricter form. The unit of translation from Week 3 matters, because legal sentences are long and their sub-clauses are separately operative. The lexical problems from Week 4 matter, because legal terms are often false friends across systems. The terminology list from Weeks 9 and 13 is now mandatory. And the cultural awareness of Week 7 matters most of all: legal concepts are institutional, and an institution that does not exist in the target legal system cannot simply be given a target-language name.

Check yourself
How can one legal text be both informative and directive?
Because it addresses two audiences. Sager’s point is that legal texts are informative for the general reader — who learns what the law is — and directive for the specific group whose conduct the text governs. Both functions live in the same words.
A convention article reads: ‘Each Contracting Party shall give notice… within three months’. Which function is operating, and what follows for your Arabic?
Directive. The sentence imposes a duty with a deadline, so the Arabic must impose a duty with a deadline — not report that notice is given, and not soften shall into a future. The time limit and the addressee must both survive exactly.
Legal translation is specialised, yet it shares characteristics with general translation. What does that mean practically?
That the methods from Weeks 2–4 still apply — you are still choosing between literal and free, still working unit by unit. What the genre removes is latitude: creative style has no place, so the workable range narrows to the precise end of the axis.
Why is a legal term more dangerous than a scientific term?
Because scientific concepts are largely shared across languages, while legal concepts are institutional and belong to a particular legal system. A term may have a familiar-looking equivalent that names a different institution — the cultural problem of Week 7, with legal consequences.
