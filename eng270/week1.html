Reading check
According to Ghazala’s Introduction, what does translation transfer, and what does it explicitly not prioritise?
Meaning — not words, grammar, style, or sound in isolation. Meaning is the composite of all four elements together, so a translation that preserves grammar or sound at the expense of meaning has failed at the one thing translation is for.
Name the four routes Ghazala gives for a foreign item to enter Arabic, with one example each.
Ready equivalent (speak → يتكلم), coinage (satellite → قمر صناعي), transliteration (aspirin → أسبرين), naturalisation (democracy → ديمقراطية).
What are the three ‘faces’ of translation Ghazala distinguishes in the Introduction?
Process (what the translator does), product (the finished translated text), and field of study (the theory and scholarship around translation).
Why does Ghazala’s brief historical sketch matter pedagogically, beyond just dates?
It repositions Arabic at the centre of translation history — via the Abbasid translation movement — rather than treating Arabic as peripheral to a Eurocentric history of the discipline.

Core ideas

The six abbreviations you will meet in every later week

Translation talk is compressed. Six pairs carry most of it, and they are used from Week 1 onward without being spelled out again:

  • SL — source language · TL — target language
  • ST — source text · TT — target text
  • SC — source culture · TC — target culture

In this course the SL is almost always English and the TL is almost always Arabic. The third pair — culture — is the one students forget, and it is the one that causes most of the difficulty from Week 5 onwards.

What translation is

Translation refers to all the processes and methods used to render or transfer the meaning of a source-language text into the target language as closely, completely and accurately as possible. That definition is doing real work: it admits that perfect transfer is not available, and it sets closeness, completeness and accuracy as the three things you are always trading against one another.

The transfer itself happens along four routes. Which route you take is a decision, not an accident — and the table below is the whole of Week 1 in miniature.

What we translate: meaning, and only meaning

We translate neither grammar, nor words, nor style, nor sounds. We always translate one thing: meaning.

That is not a slogan. Meaning is a network built out of syntax (grammar), vocabulary (words), style and phonology (sounds) taken together. Anything not relevant to meaning is normally disregarded — with one standing exception: in poetry and certain other texts, sound and prosody can matter more than propositional meaning, which is why the literary weeks are handled separately.

Three ways of looking at the same word

The word translation names three different things, and the specification asks you to keep them apart:

  • Process — what a translator actually does: reading, segmenting into units, choosing, checking, revising. This is what Weeks 2–4 analyse.
  • Product — the finished target text, which can be judged, compared with other versions, and marked. This is what your assignments are.
  • Field of study — the academic discipline that describes and theorises both of the above. This is why the course cites Newmark, Venuti, Baker and Graedler rather than simply telling you what to do.

A brief historical account

Translation is older than any theory of it. Multilingual decrees such as the Rosetta Stone show state translation at work in antiquity. The great Abbasid translation movement in ninth-century Baghdad carried Greek philosophy, medicine and mathematics into Arabic, and the Toledo translators later carried much of that same material from Arabic into Latin — Arabic sits in the middle of the transmission, not at its edge.

In Europe, the translation of scripture into vernaculars turned translation into a public and sometimes dangerous act, and produced the first sustained arguments about literal versus free rendering — the argument you will meet in Week 2 in its modern form.

Translation only became an academic field in the twentieth century, with the growth of professional and institutional translation and, from the 1970s, the naming of Translation Studies as a discipline in its own right. The historical point for this course is simple: the choices you will be making in Week 12 are old choices, and they have names.

Check yourself
A classmate says ‘translation means replacing English words with Arabic words’. What is wrong with that?
It names the wrong unit and the wrong object. We do not transfer words, we transfer meaning — and meaning is carried by syntax, vocabulary, style and phonology together. Word-replacement is in fact a specific (and risky) method, word-for-word translation, which you meet in Week 2.
Classify each of these: ‘computer’ → كمبيوتر and ‘fast food’ → وجبات سريعة.
The first is transliteration / naturalised borrowing — the English word is carried across in Arabic letters. The second uses ready Arabic material to build the equivalent. You will meet both again in Week 9 under their technical names, borrowing and loan translation.
Give one example of translation as a product and one of translation as a process.
Product: the Arabic version of a novel sitting on a shelf — a finished object you can evaluate. Process: the twenty minutes you spend deciding how to render one sentence of it, checking a dictionary, and revising your first attempt.
Why does the course insist on SC and TC and not only SL and TL?
Because a large share of translation difficulty is cultural rather than linguistic. From Week 5 on, the problems that stop you — idioms, proverbs, culture-bound objects, religious and social assumptions — sit in the gap between source culture and target culture, not between two grammars.
