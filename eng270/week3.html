Core ideas

Free translation, and the sentence that keeps it honest

Free translation is associated with translating the spirit or the message — not the letter or the form of the text. It is not committed to translating every single word.

That freedom is bounded by one rule, and it is the most quoted line in the chapter: the translator cannot translate the way he likes, but the way he understands. Freedom of method is not licence to invent. Everything you produce must still be recoverable from the source text.

The method splits in two by distance from context: the first type stays close and relates to context directly; the second goes far and relates to it only indirectly.

1 · Bound free translation

Derived from the context in a direct way, though it may go outside it somewhat — often in the form of exaggeration. This is the type most people mean when they say ‘free translation’.

Its renderings travel a long way from the words, but not away from the text: they are still bound to context. This is the type that sits closest to creative translation, and it is where Arabic proverbs and idioms earn their place.

2 · Loose free translation

This method goes far away from the direct linguistic context into inferences. It is based on the translator’s conclusions about what the speaker or writer wants to say — in other words, it is the translation of intentions, a version of pragmatic translation.

The renderings do not relate directly to the original context; they are concluded from it. Two consequences follow, and both matter for your marks. First, different translators will reach different conclusions, because these are personal conclusions rather than context-restricted transfers. Second, the interpretation beyond the text is normally the reader’s job in both languages — which is why loose free translation is so often the wrong choice.

The process of translation, and the unit

The process of translation is the organised stages of translating in application: we proceed unit by unit.

A unit of translation is any word or group of words that can give either a small or a large part of the meaning of a sentence. Its upper limit is fixed: the largest unit of translation is the sentence.

Practically, this is the habit the practical sessions are training. You do not translate a text; you segment it, decide the method for each unit, render it, then check the unit against the whole. Weeks 5–15 apply that habit to five different genres.

Check yourself
Bound free translation is often described as ‘not loose, not without limitations’. Limited by what?
By context. Bound free renderings may go outside the wording — often by exaggeration — but they remain derived from the context in a direct way. That is precisely what distinguishes them from loose free translation, which moves into inference.
Why is ‘It is half past nine’ → علينا بالمغادرة a weak translation even though it may be what the speaker meant?
It translates an intention rather than an utterance. The conclusion is the translator’s, not the text’s, and another translator would draw a different one. The interpretation beyond the words should be left to the reader in both languages.
What is the largest unit of translation?
The sentence. Anything larger is the text, and the text is translated as a sequence of units — which is why the process is described as proceeding unit by unit.
A student renders every English proverb with an Arabic proverb, in a legal contract. What has gone wrong?
Method has been chosen without regard to text type. Bound free translation is right for proverbs in literary or general prose; legal texts (Weeks 14–15) demand precision and admit almost no creative freedom. Method follows genre — that is the whole logic of the second half of this course.
