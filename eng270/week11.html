Reading check
Give the lesson’s example of one event attracting two different labels depending on who is speaking.
The same armed action can be called a ‘holy war’ by one side and a ‘guerrilla war’ by the other, depending on the speaker’s allegiance and sympathies.
What does the lesson recommend a translator do with a contested political or military term, rather than silently choosing a side?
Footnote the contested term, flagging that it carries a particular allegiance or framing, rather than adjudicating between the two labels as if one were neutral.
Why is this footnoting practice specifically important in conflict reporting, more than in most other genres on the course?
Because the same underlying event can be described in mutually exclusive ways depending on political allegiance, so silently picking one label is itself a political act — footnoting keeps the translator’s role mediating rather than adjudicating.
Why does the lesson warn that uprising-related vocabulary (Lesson 26) needs historical grounding, not just a dictionary?
Post-2011 political vocabulary is often specific to a particular country’s events and history, so a term correct for one country’s uprising may be misleading if applied to another’s.
What does the lesson recommend for keeping party and opposition labels accurate in election-related translation (Lesson 27)?
Maintaining a glossary of party and opposition labels rather than improvising translations on the fly, since these labels diverge between English and Arabic usage.
How does Arabic disaster reporting (Lesson 28) often differ in register from its English source?
It frequently carries a religious or sentimental framing that is absent from the English original — a register shift the translator should notice and decide how to handle rather than import automatically.

Core ideas

Media in the translation profession

With regard to the profession, media texts require more interpreting than written translation — and interpreting is usually more complicated and harder to tackle, because of difficulties of setting, time, voice and participants.

As for written translation, media texts can be translated more easily than complex genres such as literary texts. In translating lexis, the strategies are restricted to using a borrowed term, either transliterated or translated literally, because media terms tend to be restricted in meaning and cannot normally carry connotative senses such as metaphor or metonymy. Piggyback, for example, has straightforward equivalents in other languages.

That characteristic makes media texts a successful candidate for machine translation: the language of the media can be anticipated, much like other technical uses. Media texts could even be called a category of technical texts, if we treat the media as a field rather than strictly a discipline.

Conflicts and civil wars

Translating texts about conflicts and wars remains one of the most complex tasks a translator faces, because the register used may be interpreted differently by different parties to the conflict. What one side calls a holy war, another calls a guerrilla war.

These ideological differences in how terms are approached and actions labelled make a representative translation genuinely difficult. The translator must take cultural, social and political aspects of both source and target text into account, and be very aware of the differences, in order to produce a balanced translation.

Where terminology is controversial, the translator may resort to footnotes to explain or contextualise key source-language terms, so that the target audience understands what underlies them. Recall the Week 7 example: Thousands were killed in the raid can be rendered with a neutral verb or with one that confers a status the English does not confer. That is not a stylistic choice.

Natural disasters

As with other genres, this type of text requires good knowledge of both cultural contexts, since texts may be influenced by cultural and religious beliefs that different audiences receive differently.

The Arabic culture is religious-oriented, and this is often evident in texts about natural disasters, since most Arabs and Muslims believe that disasters are an act of God. That may not be the case in other cultures, so the translator should exercise extra caution in conveying the meaning of the source text to the target audience.

Awareness of the recipients’ cultural beliefs and values is vital. The Arabic style in this genre can be very informative while also employing religious and sentimental lexis — and a translation into English that reproduces that lexis literally will read as editorialising.

Uprisings, revolutions, elections

Lesson 26 · Uprisings and revolutions

The 2011 uprisings across the Middle East coined new terminology and phrases that have since entered the Arabic glossary and culture, and which some translators will not be familiar with. Part of that register is specific to individual countries and groups.

Two demands follow. The translator must be aware of the new register and of the internal or local context attached to it, in order to produce a representative and accurate rendering. And because much of this register is culture-specific, the historical context can prove essential — some terms are tied directly to national and regional issues that belong to the source context alone.

Lesson 27 · Elections and oppositions

Translating election texts is often considered straightforward and in reality is not, for translators with little knowledge of the target-text culture: some election terminology is interpreted differently depending on the context of the source.

In Arabic, different groups and opposition parties are given different labels and attributions, and a translator unaware of those political distinctions will misidentify the actors. The strategy the textbook recommends is unglamorous and effective: a good acquaintance with current affairs, familiarity with the registers of elections and oppositions, and a glossary built and maintained for the purpose.

Check yourself
Why are media texts described as good candidates for machine translation?
Because their language can be anticipated. Media terms tend to be restricted in meaning and do not normally carry connotative senses such as metaphor or metonymy, so lexical strategies reduce to borrowing or literal translation — the conditions machine translation handles best.
Where does that argument stop?
At loaded terminology. A machine will render a conflict term with whatever equivalent is statistically common, and in conflict reporting the choice of term is the political content. That is a judgement, and the textbook’s answer is translator awareness plus footnotes, not automation.
A source text describes a flood without any religious framing; the Arabic target register normally carries some. What do you do?
Exercise caution rather than assimilate. The Arabic style in this genre can be informative while employing religious and sentimental lexis, but importing that framing into a text that did not have it changes the source’s claim. Awareness of the recipients’ beliefs is required — adopting them on the author’s behalf is not.
Why is interpreting harder than written media translation?
Because of the setting, the time available, the voice, and the participants — none of which the interpreter controls, and all of which the written translator can work around.
