Are first impressions accurate?

Unit 1 Listening & Speaking Lab — observe carefully, challenge assumptions, weigh evidence, and make a qualified judgment.

Week 3: complete Unit 1 practice

Every task returns to one idea: a first impression can be useful, but it is incomplete evidence—not a final verdict. The recording and activities are original supplementary material.

1. Start with your experience 8–10 min

Discuss before listening.

  1. What do people notice first when they meet someone new?
  2. Describe a first impression that later changed. What evidence changed it?
  3. Can silence mean that a person is unfriendly? Give two other explanations.
  4. Which is more reliable: one strong impression or repeated behavior over time?

2. Language for careful judgment 10 min

observationa fact you can check
interpretationan explanation of meaning
assumptionan idea accepted without proof
biasa tendency affecting judgment
contextthe situation around an event
evidenceinformation supporting a claim
provisionaltemporary and open to change
stereotypea simplified belief about a group
Observation or interpretation?
  1. “Salma arrived ten minutes late.”
  2. “Salma does not respect the group.”
  3. “Rami asked three critical questions.”
  4. “Rami is a negative person.”

1 O · 2 I · 3 O · 4 I. Interpretations may be reasonable, but they need evidence.

4. Check your listening 15–20 min

From general understanding to detail, inference, and evaluation.

A. Gist
1. The speakers mainly argue that first impressions…
2. An observation…

B. Details
  1. What behavior does Salma show? Record two details.
  2. Give two possible explanations for it.
  3. What early impression does the team form about Hana or Rami?
  4. What later evidence changes it?
  5. List two fairer-judgment strategies.
Check guide
Accept accurate notes. Essential ideas: late arrival/quietness/limited participation; shyness, unfamiliarity, transport or another problem; Hana’s notes become valuable and Rami’s questions improve evidence; separate observation from interpretation, consider context and alternatives, use criteria, gather repeated evidence, and seek another perspective.
C. Think further
  1. Why can an early judgment feel convincing when evidence is weak?
  2. Would a checklist remove all bias? Why or why not?
  3. What would make you revise a positive first impression?

5. Speaking workshop: evidence, not labels 25–35 min

Make a claim, support it, acknowledge a limitation, and respond respectfully.

Round 1 — Observation or interpretation?
Choose a campus situation: someone arrives late, speaks very little, answers quickly, or avoids eye contact. Partner A makes only observations. Partner B gives two possible interpretations. Switch.
Round 2 — Four corners
Position: strongly agree / agree / disagree / strongly disagree. “In an interview, the first two minutes are enough to judge a candidate.” Give one reason, one example, and one limitation.
Round 3 — Paired role-play
A: quick judgments save time. B: quick judgments often create unfair decisions. Discuss Salma for two minutes. Use four target words and: I see your point, but… / What evidence supports that? / Another explanation could be… / I would revise my view if…
Round 4 — Academic response
In groups of three, prepare a 90-second answer. Include a qualified claim, example, counterargument, and recommendation.

6. Exit task 5 min

Are first impressions accurate? Write or record 90–120 words. Give a qualified answer, use four target words, and include one idea from the recording plus one personal or campus example.
