Are first impressions accurate?

A 3.5-hour intensive listening, note-taking and speaking workshop.

Today’s objective

By the end of the workshop, you will make a qualified answer to the Unit 1 question. You will distinguish observation from interpretation, recognize bias and context, take organized notes from four listening sections, and support a spoken opinion with evidence.

1. Quick first impressions 10 min

  1. What do people notice first: appearance, voice, behavior, confidence, or something else?
  2. Describe an impression that later changed. What new information changed it?
  3. Finish this sentence three ways: “A first impression may be useful when …”

2. Language sort: facts or conclusions? 10 min

observationa fact that can be checked
interpretationan explanation of meaning
assumptionan idea accepted without proof
biasa tendency affecting judgment
contextthe situation around an event
evidenceinformation supporting a claim
provisionaltemporary and open to change
stereotypea simplified group belief
Sort and explain: “Hana wrote notes.” / “Hana is passive.” / “Rami asked critical questions.” / “Rami is negative.” Which are observations? Which are interpretations? What evidence would you need before accepting each conclusion?
Check
Observations: Hana wrote notes; Rami asked questions. Interpretations: Hana is passive; Rami is negative. A conclusion needs repeated, relevant evidence and context.

3. Listening 1 — Debate: the main argument 5:48 · 15 min

Listen once with no pausing. Write only the big question, each speaker’s position, and their shared conclusion.

4. Listening 1 — detail hunt 5:48 · 20 min

Listen again. Stop only after each named case.

  1. Record two observations about Salma.
  2. Write two alternative interpretations of her behavior.
  3. What first impression does the group form about Hana or Rami?
  4. What later evidence changes that impression?
  5. Which strategy for fairer judgment is most practical for university group work?
Self-check guide
Look for observation versus interpretation, context, alternative explanations, repeated evidence, criteria, and another perspective. Your wording may differ from a partner’s.

6. Listening 2 — evidence and provisional judgment Deep Dive 0:00–7:00 · 25 min

Listen to the first section of the extended discussion. Focus on how a judgment becomes more reliable.

Claim about first impressions
Evidence that strengthens a judgment
Evidence that is weak or irrelevant
New vocabulary in context
Pair reconstruction: Use only your notes to explain the meaning of provisional. Then give a new campus example of a provisional judgment.

7. Listening 3 — bias, stereotype, and context Deep Dive 7:00–15:00 · 30 min

This time, make a cause-and-effect map. What causes an unfair conclusion? What can interrupt it?

Cause → interpretation → possible consequence → correction
  1. Choose one example from the listening.
  2. Map the initial observation and the assumption behind it.
  3. Explain a possible unfair consequence.
  4. State one correction: context, criteria, repeated evidence, or another perspective.
Jigsaw: Group A becomes experts on bias. Group B becomes experts on stereotypes. Group C becomes experts on context. Regroup: each expert teaches the other two groups in 60 seconds.

8. Listening 3 — inference workshop 15 min

  1. Why can two observers see the same event but reach different conclusions?
  2. Why can a positive stereotype still cause harm?
  3. Can criteria eliminate all bias? Give a qualified answer.
  4. Which correction would work best before a group project begins? Defend your choice.

9. Listening 4 — reaching a qualified conclusion Deep Dive 15:00–23:18 · 30 min

Listen for the conclusion structure: claim → limitation → recommendation.

Final claim
Limitation / uncertainty
Recommendation
Useful signpost phrase
One-minute summary: In pairs, explain the conclusion without looking at your notes after the first 30 seconds.

10. Evidence ladder 10 min

Rank these from weakest to strongest evidence for the claim “This student is reliable”: one person’s first reaction; one late arrival; a pattern of meeting deadlines; feedback from several teammates; a self-description. Compare your order with another group and defend one disagreement.

11. Speaking round — challenge an assumption 15 min

A: “A quiet classmate is not interested.” B: challenge the claim using context and alternative explanations. Use: What evidence supports that? / Another explanation could be… / I would not conclude that until… Switch after two minutes.

12. Speaking round — case conference 20 min

Case: A new group member arrives late, speaks little, and misses one message. Decide whether to give them a smaller task. Roles: facilitator, evidence checker, context researcher, and reporter. You must make a provisional decision and state what new evidence would change it.

13. Final performance and feedback 20 min

In groups of three, prepare a 90-second answer to “Are first impressions accurate?” Include a qualified claim, one listening example, one counterargument, four target words, and a recommendation. A listener uses this checklist: clear claim; relevant evidence; limitation; respectful interaction; accurate vocabulary.
Exit task: Record or write 100–130 words: “The best way to make a fair first impression is …” Include an observation, an interpretation, and the evidence you would need before making a final judgment.
