1. Debate — the two positions 5:48 CORE 30 min

Goal: identify what each speaker argues, and separate what was observed from what was concluded.

Stage 1 — Learn the words

provisionaltemporary; open to being changed later“A provisional judgement” — a judgement you expect to revise.
hypothesisan idea you test, not a conclusion you keepThe speakers end by calling a first impression a hypothesis.
observationa fact anyone in the room could check“She arrived ten minutes late.”
interpretationthe meaning or motive you add to a fact“She is careless.”
confirmation biasnoticing only what fits what you already believeThe same mistake is read differently depending on expectation.
to anchorto get stuck on your first idea and not revise it“We don’t revise — we anchor.”

All six words are heard in this recording.

Quick match — which word means “a fact anyone could check”?

An observation is verifiable. An interpretation adds a reason or a judgement to it.
Complete the sentence: “Treat it as ______ — you may change it tomorrow.”

Provisional means temporary and open to revision — the key word of the whole unit.

Stage 2 — Prepare

You will hear the real figure in the first ten seconds.

Stage 3 — Listen and take notes

Listening 1 · Debate · 5:48

First listen: no writing except keywords — just get the two positions. Second listen: fill the frame below. Identify the speakers by their argument, not by their voice: one is the sceptic, one is the pragmatist.

Note style — copy this shape, not these words:
late 10 min → “careless”? = interpretation, not obs.
Keywords, arrows and abbreviations. Do not write sentences.
What is wrong with first impressions?
Why are they still worth having?
Two or three, in keywords.
One line.
Keep your first notes above. Add here in a different form, so you can see what you missed the first time.

Stage 4 — Answer

Main ideas first, then details. Leave an answer blank rather than guessing — blank and wrong are reported differently.

1. Which statement best expresses what the two speakers finally agree on?

Both speakers move to this position at the end. Neither “always accurate” nor “never use them” is what they settle on.
2. What do the two speakers actually disagree about?

Both accept that the judgements happen automatically. The argument is about whether their usefulness outweighs their distortion.
3. How quickly does the brain judge a stranger’s trustworthiness?

Stated in the opening line — before you have consciously processed the face.
4. Complete: “The brain uses shortcuts in order to reduce ______.”

We cannot analyse every new person deeply, so the brain economises — but as the other speaker replies, reducing load is not the same as finding the truth.
5. In the project-team example, what happened to the person who spoke confidently at the start?

His early confidence was read as leadership. The evidence that arrived later did not support that reading.
6. What did the quiet note-taker in that team actually contribute?

The behaviour that looked like passivity produced the decisive work.
7. In the Salma example, which one is the observation?

Anyone in the room could check the clock. The other two are stories the brain generates about why.

Evidence check — true, false, or not stated?

8. One speaker compares a first impression to the predictive text on a phone.

True — the brain takes one input and auto-fills the rest of the personality.
9. The speakers agree that first impressions should never be used.

False — they agree an impression is a starting hypothesis, not that it is worthless.

Stage 5 — Check and revise

Write 2–3 sentences. This is not auto-marked — you will compare it with a model answer.
Show a model answer and self-check list (open it only after you have written)
Model answer. Calling a judgement “provisional” only helps if you actually go back to it. The speaker’s point is that in practice people do not update — the first judgement becomes the anchor, and later evidence gets bent to fit it. So the word “provisional” describes an intention, not what the brain naturally does.

Self-check — tick what your answer has: ☐ says what “anchor” means here ☐ links it to the word provisional ☐ explains the consequence (later evidence gets distorted) ☐ uses your own wording.

Your wording will differ from the model, and that is expected. Judge your answer on the ideas above, not on matching the sentences.

Your partner ticks: did you give the fact, the story, and the reason the story is not yet proved?
Useful language: “What she actually did was…” · “The conclusion they jumped to was…” · “That is an interpretation because…” · “Before I accepted that, I would want…”
Extension HOMEWORK
The speakers name the university where the research was done.

Not stated — the finding is cited, but no institution is named. “Not stated” is different from “false”: the recording simply does not tell us.
Model answer
Predictive text does not only guess — it learns and adapts from thousands of patterns, so an experienced interviewer’s impression is trained, not random. The reply is that those thousands of past interactions were themselves filtered through human bias, so more experience can mean a better-practised bias rather than a more accurate judgement.

2. Evidence and provisional judgement 7:00 CORE 30 min

Goal: judge how strong a piece of evidence actually is, and what it is evidence of.

Where we are: this recording opens a longer discussion. It begins with a scene in a busy coffee shop.

Stage 1 — Learn the words

impressiona very fast early judgement about someoneBuilt from surface behaviour, appearance, or something you were told.
verifiableable to be checked by someone elseThe time on the clock is verifiable.
provisionaltemporary; a draft, open to revisionThe hosts want this word as a “warning label”.
patternsomething repeated across situationsHumans are described as pattern-recognition machines.
shortcuta quick mental route that saves effortThe brain is an energy-saving organ.
second-handinformation from someone else, not observed by youGossip whispered before a meeting.

All six are heard in this recording.

Which word describes information you did not observe yourself?

Second-hand information reaches you through someone else, so it carries their interpretation as well as the facts.

Stage 2 — Prepare

Be honest — you will compare your three words with the recording.

Stage 3 — Listen and take notes

Listening 2 · Evidence · 7:00

First listen: main argument only. Second listen: one strong and one weak kind of evidence. Headings only this time — you decide the layout.

Stage 4 — Answer

1. What is the coffee-shop scene there to show?

The point is the speed and the completeness of the profile, not the stranger’s behaviour.
2. What plan do the hosts announce for the discussion?

They set out this four-part structure explicitly near the start — and they follow it across Listenings 2, 3 and 4.
3. In the coffee-shop scene, what has the listener’s brain already decided?

A complete psychological profile, built from a sigh and a bag.
4. How much had the stranger spoken at that point?

This is the hosts’ emphasis: zero speech, complete profile.
5. Complete: “The brain is an ______ organ that wants to take shortcuts.”

It cannot process every detail of every person, so it economises by default.
6. Which two situations show people who have no time for a full assessment?

Both are genuine constraints on time and information — not laziness.
7. What single word do the hosts want as a “warning label” on every first impression?

Provisional — temporary, a draft, fully open to revision as new information arrives.

Evidence check — true, false, or not stated?

8. The hosts say first impressions are always a bad thing.

False — they argue impressions serve a vital survival and social purpose. The danger is the weight we give them.
9. A security camera is used as an image for objective observation.

True — the camera has no emotion and no agenda; it records movement in time and space, and nothing else.

Stage 5 — Check and revise

Show a model answer and self-check list
Model answer. It is offered as a description of how the world actually works — if people act on their perception, that perception has real effects whether or not it is accurate. But it is immediately pressure-tested, so it functions as a warning: the fact that a judgement gets treated as reality is exactly why the judgement needs better evidence, not less.

Self-check: ☐ says what the phrase means ☐ takes a position ☐ gives a reason drawn from the recording ☐ acknowledges the other reading.

Judging someone from limited information is compared to watching an entire film.

3. Assumption, context, bias and stereotype 8:00 CORE 30 min

Goal: explain how the same observed behaviour can produce opposite conclusions.

Where we are: this section continues straight on from Listening 2. The hosts are still discussing Salma, and are about to move on to assumption, context, bias and stereotype. It begins mid-conversation — that is expected, not a fault in the recording.

Stage 1 — Learn the words

assumptiona mental bridge over a gap in your knowledge, accepted without proof“Charming in an interview = a good employee.”
contextthe surrounding circumstances that give an event its meaningCalled the “invisible background”.
biasan ingrained tendency pushing you toward one conclusion, mostly unconsciousIt changes which details you even notice.
stereotypea simplified belief applied to all members of a groupBroader and blunter than a bias.
objectivebased on checkable fact, independent of the observerThe clock time she walked in.
subjectiveshaped by the observer’s own view“A disrespectful slacker.”

All six are heard and defined in this recording.

Which word is defined as “a mental bridge we build over a gap in knowledge”?

An assumption connects an observation to a conclusion without proof — the bridge you did not notice you crossed.
Complete: “Bias and stereotypes work like a ______ on a camera.”

A filter changes the picture and changes which details you notice at all — bias operates before interpretation, at the level of attention.

Stage 2 — Prepare

Stage 3 — Listen and take notes

Listening 3 · Bias and context · 8:00

Second listen: fill the map below for one example from the recording. Use arrows and keywords; write a full sentence only in the last box.

What could a camera record?
What bridge was crossed?
What unfair result could follow?
Context, criteria, more evidence, or another perspective?

Stage 4 — Answer

1. What is the central claim about context?

The observation stays fixed; the meaning moves. That is why context is treated as the missing half of every first impression.
2. How is a bias different from a stereotype here?

Bias is a tilt in how you judge. A stereotype is a belief applied to every member of a group — broader, and the hosts call it a step further.
3. Two reasons for arriving late are contrasted. What stays exactly the same?

Sleeping through an alarm and a bus breaking down produce an identical observation. Only the context — and therefore the meaning — differs.
4. What cultural explanation is offered for avoiding eye contact?

The behaviour may mean the opposite of what the observer assumed. This is the clearest case in the unit of context reversing a meaning.
5. Besides boredom, what two explanations of silence are given?

The second explanation applies directly to this class — silence in a second language is often processing, not absence.
6. In the two-employees example, what is identical and what differs?

The same minor mistake becomes a “temporary blip” for the favoured employee and “proof of permanent weakness” for the other. Same evidence, opposite conclusions.

Evidence check — true, false, or not stated?

7. The hosts say bias usually operates consciously.

False — they stress it operates almost entirely unconsciously. That is what makes it hard to correct by good intentions alone.
8. The two employees are given names.

Not stated — they are referred to only as one employee and the other. “Not stated” means the recording never tells us, which is different from telling us it is untrue.

Stage 5 — Check and revise

Show a model answer and self-check list
Model answer. It is real, direct evidence of the candidate’s communication in one controlled setting on one day — the hosts accept that fully. What it does not show is whether the person can handle a high-pressure crisis, sustain the daily work, or fit the existing team. The error is not believing the evidence; it is stretching evidence about one narrow thing into a conclusion about everything.

Self-check: ☐ names what the evidence does support ☐ names at least two things it does not ☐ uses the idea of a limited observation window ☐ does not simply say “first impressions are wrong”.

Stage 6 — Discuss

Jigsaw (10 min). Group A: experts on bias. Group B: experts on stereotypes. Group C: experts on context. Prepare a 60-second explanation with one example from the recording and one from your own experience. Then re-group so each new group has one expert of each kind, and teach each other.
Useful language: “The definition we were given is…” · “The example in the recording was…” · “The way you would notice it is…” · “One way to interrupt it is…”
Extension HOMEWORK
The job-candidate example is presented as genuine evidence of something.

True — it is explicitly called direct, tangible evidence. The unit is not arguing that observation is worthless; it is arguing about what observation licenses you to conclude.
Model answer
Where information is missing, the mind does not leave a blank — it writes a story to fill the gap, and then treats that invented story as fact. The practical response is to notice the gap and name it (“I do not know why she was late”) instead of resolving it, and to delay the conclusion until the missing context actually arrives.

4. A qualified conclusion 8:18 CORE 30 min

Goal: build the qualified answer you will use in your spoken performance, and take away three usable strategies.

Where we are: this final section begins mid-answer. The hosts are explaining why even a positive stereotype can cause harm, and then move to a full case study and their conclusion.

Stage 1 — Learn the words

characteristica typical feature or quality of someone’s behaviourBeing quiet, asking questions, making a schedule.
perceptionthe way we understand or think about somethingA stereotype warps perception before judgement begins.
criteriathe standards you decide to judge bySet them before you evaluate someone.
hypothesisan idea held in order to be testedThe final recommendation of the whole discussion.
qualifiedstated with its limits made clear“Sometimes accurate — but only within limits.”
to synthesiseto combine separate ideas into one coherent wholeWhat the quiet note-taker did for her team.

All six are heard in this recording.

A qualified answer is one that…

A qualified answer is not a vague answer. It commits to a position and says where that position stops being safe.

Stage 2 — Prepare

Stage 3 — Listen and take notes

Listening 4 · Qualified conclusion · 8:18

No headings this time — you decide the structure. Aim for three lines you could speak from.

Suggested shape: claim / limitation / recommendation — but organise it your own way.

Stage 4 — Answer

1. What is the final, qualified answer to “Are first impressions accurate?”

Notice the shape of the answer: a position, then its limits. That is what “qualified” means, and it is what your spoken answer needs today.
2. What framework do the hosts finally recommend?

A hypothesis, not a verdict. This single sentence is the takeaway of the whole unit.
3. Match each team member to the label the group gave in the first hour.

These are the snap judgements of the first hour — not what the four people turned out to be.
4. Now match each person to what the evidence showed three weeks later.

Every single first-hour label was reversed by three weeks of evidence. That reversal is the argument of the whole unit in one story.
5. Were the visible characteristics wrong?

This is the sharpest point in the unit. Hana was quiet; Rami did question everything. Accurate observation still produced a false conclusion.
6. Which three practical strategies are recommended?

These three are the transferable skills of the lesson — usable in a group project next week.
7. Complete: “Give them time to show you the rest of the ______, not just the screenshot.”

A callback to the screenshot image from Listening 2 — the discussion closes the loop it opened.

Evidence check — true, false, or not stated?

8. The project team had five members.

False — four students are described: Faisal, Hana, Rami and Lila.
9. The hosts state the grade the team finally received.

Not stated — the outcome is described in terms of who contributed what, never as a grade.

Stage 5 — Check and revise

Show a model answer and self-check list
Model answer (using Rami). In one meeting Rami questioned every idea. That is a narrow, situational behaviour: it describes one hour of one meeting. The team converted it into a permanent claim about who he is — “a negative, toxic person.” Three weeks later the same behaviour stopped them presenting a false claim to their professor. The behaviour never changed; only the evidence about what it was for changed.

Self-check: ☐ names the observed behaviour ☐ names the permanent label the group applied ☐ shows what the later evidence revealed ☐ makes clear the observation itself was never wrong.

Stage 6 — Discuss

Groups — an evidence-based recommendation (8 min). A new member of your group project arrives late, speaks little, and misses one message. Decide whether to give them a smaller task. Roles: facilitator, evidence checker, context researcher, reporter. You must reach a provisional decision and state exactly what new evidence would change it.
Useful language: “Our provisional decision is…” · “The evidence we actually have is…” · “We would change our mind if…” · “That is an assumption rather than an observation.”
Extension HOMEWORK
Setting criteria is compared to a teacher writing a marking rubric before reading essays.

True — and it is worth noticing that this is exactly what your own teacher does with your work.
Model answer
Context and purpose. The identical behaviour — challenging every idea — can serve obstruction or quality control. In an academic research task, pressure-testing claims before they reach the professor protects the team. The behaviour alone does not tell you which it is; what it is for in that setting does.
