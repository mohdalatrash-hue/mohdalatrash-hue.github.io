Narrative Voice & the Child Reader

Before a word is translated, hear who is telling the story — and to whom. Based on Lathey (2016), Ch. 1.

1 · Narrative Voice 2 · Dialogue & Dialect 3 · Multimodality & Sound

🗣️ Telling a story TO a child

Lathey: writing (and translating) for children is "adult-to-child communication." Oittinen calls it an imaginary dialogue with the child reader — the translator must reach the child of the target culture. Your first job is to preserve that relationship, not just the words.

📖
Required reading for this workshop: Lathey (2016), “Narrative in Children’s Literature” (Ch. 1). Everything below practises the narrator’s voice and address to the child reader.
The storytelling voice is tuned to a young reader — its warmth, simplicity, humour and rhythm are part of the meaning. Flatten it into neutral adult prose and the book stops speaking to children.
Barbara Wall (1991) distinguished how narrators address the reader: single address (speaking to the child alone), double address (winking over the child's head to adults), and the older distant, didactic narrator who lectures or "civilizes." The translator must keep the same stance.
Translate the voice, not just the sense. Keep the narrator's relationship with the child — the direct address, the warmth, the rhythm. Type your version, then reveal the notes.
Required reading guide: Lathey (2016), “Narrative in Children’s Literature” (Ch. 1). Work through each section and use the questions to prepare for class discussion.

1. Narrative communication with the child reader

  1. How does translation for children create a dialogue among the author, translator, and child reader?
  2. What kinds of knowledge about childhood help a translator address young readers convincingly?
  3. How can a narrative voice be didactic, subversive, conspiratorial, or directed at both children and adults?

2. Dual address in children’s literature

  1. What signals show that a passage is addressing adults as well as children?
  2. What is lost when a translator simplifies a joke or ambiguity that younger readers may not fully understand?
  3. How could a translator preserve two different readings without making either one too explicit?

3. Narrative voice

  1. How do pronouns, asides, punctuation, or italics position the narrator in relation to the child reader?
  2. When a language cannot reproduce a shift in narrative perspective directly, what compensatory devices are available?
  3. Why might an author’s preface or biography matter when translating narrative voice?

4. Child narrators

  1. Which features make a child narrator sound convincing: vocabulary, syntax, punctuation, repetition, or something else?
  2. How can a translator preserve emotional intensity without caricaturing child speech?
  3. What target-language books could serve as useful voice models for a narrator of the same age and social background?

5. The translator’s voice: interventions

  1. What information is essential for comprehension, and what can safely remain unfamiliar or implicit?
  2. Should an explanation appear inside the story, in a preface, or in an afterword—and how would each placement affect the reading experience?
  3. How can the translator’s added voice blend with the narrator without falsely appearing to be the author’s own words?

6. Censorship and ideological messages

  1. Which changes protect a child, and which merely reflect adult discomfort or cultural prejudice?
  2. What literary or ideological effects are lost when a troubling detail is softened or removed?
  3. How should a translator respond when a publisher, state body, or religious authority requests censorship?

7. Narrative communication and reader response theory

  1. How would an aesthetic first reading change the decisions made during an efferent, problem-solving reading?
  2. Which gaps in a children’s text invite productive inference, and which create avoidable confusion across cultures?
  3. What kind of evidence from actual child readers would be most useful when revising a translation?

8. Stylistic and linguistic issues

  1. How can syntax be made accessible without flattening rhythm, variation, or stylistic experimentation?
  2. What cultural assumptions enter a translation when an animal must be assigned a gendered pronoun?
  3. Which features of the target writing system must be adjusted for the readers’ age and literacy level?

9. Tense

  1. How does present versus past tense change immediacy, suspense, and the child listener’s sense of witnessing events?
  2. When should target-language convention outweigh the source text’s tense pattern?
  3. Why might picture books and comics justify a different tense strategy from a conventional prose novel?
