Sing It, Then Study It

Start by translating six songs you already know. Find out where they fight back. Then the reading explains why.

🎶 Translate for the tongue and the ear

A nursery rhyme that doesn't bounce, or a song you can't sing to the tune, has failed — however accurate. Here the rhythm, rhyme and sound-play matter as much as the words.

📖
Required reading for this workshop: “Translating Sounds and Reading Aloud” — Lathey (2016), Chapter 5, Translating Children's Literature. Tabs 2–5 below teach directly from this chapter; do Tab 1 before you read it.

🎤 Before you read anything — just translate.

Below are six songs almost every child knows. Translate each one into Arabic so a Saudi child could sing it — not so it reads accurately on the page. Sing your version out loud, or clap it. If it stumbles, that stumble is the lesson.

You are meant to get stuck. Every one of these six is booby-trapped in a different way, and the trap is the exact problem the chapter deals with. When you've made a real attempt, open “Where it fought back” — it will name the wall you just hit and send you to the tab that handles it. It will not hand you the answer.

⚠️ Don't open the notes before you've written something. Getting it wrong first is the point.

Reading aloud is not a check at the end — it is the design brief. Lathey opens the chapter by putting read-aloud translation in the same category as poetry: any text “where aural, aesthetic or rhetorical power is a primary consideration.” Young children hear books more often than they read them, so you are writing for a performer and a listener, not a silent reader.
Listening to books being read aloud is the only way for an illiterate child to enter the world of literature. — Oittinen (2000: 32), quoted in Lathey (2016, Ch. 5)
Danish translation scholar Cay Dollerup argues that translating for reading aloud is an art requiring great competence of translators. — Dollerup (2003: 82), quoted in Lathey (2016, Ch. 5)
The chapter's strategies for read-aloud translation
  1. Read passages of the longer text aloud — listen for awkward phrases, and for rhythms and cadences that echo the source.
  2. Read drafts aloud to children and watch how they respond.
  3. Record a draft on any device and listen back to the playback.
  4. Translate aloud, using voice-activated software.
  5. Decide whether to use the standard target-language animal cries, or keep the source language's (Tab 3).

Lathey (2016, Ch. 5), “To sum up, strategies for translating for reading aloud…”

An animal cry is a convention, not a recording. The rooster does not change when it crosses a border — the spelling does. Each language has agreed on its own imitation, and the translator has to switch from one phonetic system to another. This is the commonest trap in songs for the very young, because animal characters are everywhere in them.
The same cockerel, four languages

Lathey's point: the cry keeps its characteristic rhythmic pattern across all four — while every single sound changes. Some cries match closely across languages (a Spanish pig's oinc oinc and an English oink oink); others do not match at all (a Spanish dog's guau against an English woof woof, ruff ruff or bow-wow).

Arabic does something English doesn't

English mostly spells the noise. Arabic has both — an imitative form and a noun for the act of making the sound (اسم الصوت). You have a choice English translators don't get, and it changes the register of your line completely.

In a song for a five-year-old the imitation almost always wins — it is what the child can join in with. The sound-noun belongs to narration about the animal, not to the animal's own voice. Choose deliberately, and stay consistent across the whole song.

…but the conventional choice isn't the only one

Lathey notes that while it is common practice to use the target-language equivalent, a more playful approach might catch children's imaginations: leaving a German cockerel crying kikeriki in an English book would intrigue a child who already knows cock-a-doodle-doo. Her example is Eric Carle and Kazuo Iwamura's Where Are You Going? To See My Friend! (2001) — one book read from the front in English and from the back in Japanese, two bands of animals calling in their own languages, meeting in the middle. The foreign cry is not a mistake there; it is the whole design.

Puns and nonsense are where literal translation dies fastest. Children's authors build on idioms and puns deliberately, because discovering that a word has two meanings is an early literary pleasure — it grows the child's metalinguistic awareness. The joke is not decoration you can drop; it is part of what the book is for.
The problem, in one picture book
'Very well, but don't muck about.' 'Yes, but don't flap,' said Mr. Gumpy.— John Burningham, Mr. Gumpy's Outing (1970), discussed in Lathey (2016, Ch. 5)

Mr Gumpy tells the pig not to muck about and the chickens not to flap. Each phrase means one thing for a misbehaving passenger and another for that particular animal. The German translation gives ‘Gut. Aber mach keinen Dreck!’ (“Good. But don't make a mess!”) and ‘Ja, aber flattert nicht herum.’ (“Yes, but don't flap around.”) — the instruction survives; the double meaning does not. Ask yourself now: does Arabic give you a phrase that scolds a child and describes a pig?

On translating Asterix, Anthea Bell: […] the translation of something which depends as much as Asterix on wordplay and puns has, in any event, to be very free, often more of an adaptation, if anything like the same humorous effect is to be produced in English. — Bell (1980: 129–30), quoted in Lathey (2016, Ch. 5)
Compensation — the move that saves the joke

Bell's principle is that you need not land the joke in the same place. You must land the same kind of joke somewhere. Her famous “obvious gift”: French represents a hiccup as Hips!, English as Hic! — so drunken Roman legionaries could be made to hiccup in Latin, “Hic, haec, hoc”, a joke that does not exist in the French at all, inserted to replace one lost elsewhere.

This is the most useful idea in the chapter for your Arabic work: keep a running debt. When a pun dies, write it down, and pay it back with an Arabic pun at the next opportunity the text gives you.

The chapter's seven-step sequence for idioms and wordplay
  1. Recognise the idiom or pun in the source text in the first place.
  2. Assess its function and resonance — in the passage, and in the text as a whole.
  3. Look for an equivalent idiom or pun in the target language.
  4. If no replacement exists, consider literal translation, possibly with an explanation woven into the text — but only rarely, or you confuse and alienate the child reader.
  5. For an idiom, the likeliest option is simply to convey its meaning in the target language.
  6. Deletion of the pun or idiom is sometimes the only option.
  7. Then compensate — put an idiom or pun familiar to target-culture children somewhere else in the text.

Lathey (2016, Ch. 5), “The following sequence may assist in the translation of idioms and wordplay.” Steps 6 and 7 belong together: deletion is only acceptable if you pay the debt back.

Nonsense verse: can it be translated at all?

Umberto Eco says no — invented language, he argues, is where no translation is possible, because the phonosymbolic effect depends precisely on the absence of any semantic level. Lathey disagrees, and her evidence is Anthea Bell's English Morgenstern:

Morgenstern (German)Bell (English)
Kroklokwafzi? Semememi!Kroklokwoffzie? Seemimeemi!
Seikronto – prafriplo:Siyokronto – prufliplo:
Bifzi, bafzi; hulalemi:Biftsi baftsi; hulaleemi:
Quasti basti bo . . .quasti basti bo . . .
Lalu lalu lalu lalu la!Laloo laloo laloo laloola!

Bell changes almost nothing semantic — there is nothing semantic to change — yet she is unmistakably choosing: the German long ‘u’ becomes an English ‘oo’, and she declines the ‘v’ sound English would need to mirror the German ‘qu’, partly because how the letters look on the page is part of the pleasure. Lathey's verdict: this is translation, because Bell works between two languages and makes choices for a specific young audience.

Note for Arabic: Bell had a related alphabet and a related sound system to work with. You do not. Ask what an Arabic-reading child's eye and ear enjoy, and build the nonsense out of that — not out of transliterated English.

Decide what you are protecting before you start. Lathey: in verse for younger children the replication of musicality, sound and form is often the translator's primary concern — but sometimes metre must be sacrificed for the poetic message instead. Every choice below costs something. The professional skill is choosing the cost knowingly, not discovering it afterwards.
Singability — the test you can actually run

If the song has a known tune, the tune is a hard constraint: your Arabic must carry roughly the same number of beats per line, or the child cannot sing it. There is only one real test, and it is not silent — sing it, or clap it, against the original. A version that scans on paper and stumbles in the mouth has failed. The whole chapter rests on the claim that the ear, not the eye, judges children's verse.

The limerick — and two translators who solved it differently
There was an Old Man with a beard, Who said, "It is just as I feared!— Two Owls and a Hen, Four Larks and a Wren, Have all built their nests in my beard!"— Edward Lear (1846), public domain · AABBA

The form: five lines, AABBA, anapestic rhythm, two short central lines, comic content. Kreller (2007) collected several German versions of this exact limerick. All of them keep the rhyme scheme and the five-line shape — and still come out utterly different:

TranslatorWhat they changed to make it work
Enzensberger
(1977)		Turns Lear's Old Man into a young one (‘Jüngling’), and imports an exotic golden oriole purely so ‘Pirole’ will rhyme with ‘Dohle’ (jackdaw). Shifts out of Lear's opening iamb into a trochaic metre. The birds are no longer Lear's birds.
Artmann
(1964)		Adds a diminutive ‘pair’ of night owls (‘Pärchen’) to reach the rhyme with ‘Lerchen’ (larks), and quietly promotes four larks to five — probably to dodge the two syllables of ‘vier’. Adds a missing pair of spectacles in place of ‘There was’. Otherwise much closer to Lear.

Both are poets; both keep the form; neither keeps the content intact. The rhyme is bought, and the price is paid in birds. That is the normal economy of verse translation — and it is exactly what you will do in Arabic. Note Lathey's warning, though: Lear's poem is illustrated, and his Old Man is plainly not a youth. Enzensberger's rhyme now contradicts the picture on the page.

When rhythm is the poem — and the translation loses it
We're going on a bear hunt We're going to catch a big one. What a beautiful day! We're not scared.— Michael Rosen, We're Going on a Bear Hunt (1989: 1)

No rhyme here at all — the poem runs on a marching rhythm and on repetition (“We're” opening three of four lines; “What a beautiful day!” returning as a refrain). Lathey shows the German version losing precisely that: rearranged into short broken lines, it is difficult to read aloud with any rhythmic pattern beyond a partial rhyme. The meaning arrives; the march does not. Unrhymed verse looks like the easy case and is not — here the rhythm was the thing worth protecting, and it is the thing that got dropped.

The cost of a perfect rhyme

Sophie Hannah's English version of Tove Jansson's Moomin verse keeps the rhyme scheme beautifully — ‘nearly night / bit of light’, closing on a rhyming couplet with the trees sounding a threatening ‘hiss’. But to hold the rhyme she adds urgency and menace that Jansson never wrote: Hurrying, Quick, Don't hang around, strange creatures. In the Swedish, that threat is carried entirely by the pictures. The rhyme was saved by making the words say what the images were already saying — so the text–picture interplay was spent to buy it. Lathey calls this juggling four dimensions at once: sound, sense, form, and image.

The limerick is old, comic, and exact. Lathey describes it as a form with “five lines, aa/bb/a rhyme scheme, anapestic rhythm, short central lines and scurrilous or comic content” that has been popular in English “since the eighteenth century” (2016, Ch. 5, pp. 102–103). Every one of those constraints is fixed before you translate a single word — for a children's limerick you are filling a mould, not free-writing a poem.
Marking the shape, before you touch the words
There was an Old Man in a tree, Who was horribly bored by a Bee; When they said, "Does it buzz?" he replied, "Yes, it does!" It's a regular brute of a Bee.— Edward Lear, A Book of Nonsense (1846), public domain · AABBA

Line by line: tree (A) · Bee (A) · buzz? (B) · does! (B) · Bee (A) — the same AABBA Lathey traces through Kreller's German versions of the beard limerick in Tab 5. Count the syllables and the “short central lines” jump out: 8 / 9 / 6 / 6 / 9. And “buzz” is not incidental — it is the same onomatopoeic convention you met in Tab 3 (Animal Cries), doing double duty as a rhyme word.

Some editions typeset lines 3–4 as a single printed line to save space; the metrical structure underneath is still five lines. Before you translate any limerick, do this: mark the rhyme letters, count the syllables, find the onomatopoeia if there is one. Only then open Arabic.

Read your two versions back against Lear's. Did you keep the mould — AABBA, the short middle couplet, the anapestic gallop — even in the places where you had to change the content to make the rhyme land? That trade is the whole lesson of Tab 5, applied here to material built specifically for a child.
Same mould, older audience. Lathey's own description of the limerick names “scurrilous or comic content” as part of the form itself, not a modern addition (2016, Ch. 5, p. 102). For an adult reader the comedy leans less on vivid pictures (Tab 8) and more on tone — deadpan understatement, irony, a punchline that lands on the very last word. Translating tone without compensation is usually where these fail.
The callback ending

Lear's own favourite trick: line 5 echoes line 1's description of the person, word for word, as the punchline. Nothing new is reported — the joke is that the character is exactly as odd at the end as at the start, stated now with a flourish (“this courageous Young Lady of Norway”). It is a joke of tone, not of content, and a literal Arabic echo of the same phrase may not read as a punchline at all. This is the same problem Tab 4 calls compensation: if the callback doesn't land as written, you may need to build your own comic echo elsewhere in the Arabic line rather than translating this one literally.

One more question before you close this tab. The “Clyde” limerick below sits in a classic English subgenre — comic verse built on sudden death — within the tradition Lathey calls “scurrilous”. Would you set this exact text in front of Saudi undergraduates without comment? Does your answer change your translation, or only your decision about whether to use it at all? That distinction — translating a text versus deciding whether to assign it — is a professional judgement call, not a linguistic one.
