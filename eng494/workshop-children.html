🧸 What makes children's translation special

The translator juggles three things at once — source words, target words, and pictures (Hoole noticed this tension back in 1659). Add onomatopoeia, signs drawn inside images, and a child-friendly voice that must still be read aloud well.

Required reading for this workshop: “Multimediality, Onomatopoeia and Inscriptions.” The exercises below practise sound-words, nonsense and word–image interplay from this reading.

Multimodality: text and image make meaning together. Watch for: text the picture contradicts or completes, words drawn inside the picture (signs, labels), and cultural/visual layout (including right-to-left reading order in Arabic).
Workshop routine · Read the pictures first

Before translating any spread of a picture book, cover the words and read only the pictures. Then work through this list in pairs (10 minutes), and only afterwards draft your Arabic.

  • What do the pictures show that the words never say?
  • What do the words say that the pictures never show?
  • Where do word and picture disagree, or does the picture add irony to the text (or the text to the picture)?
  • Is any lettering drawn inside the picture (signs, labels, sound-letters)?
  • Is there anything you can count (animals, children, days) that your Arabic numbers and nouns must still match?
  • Which of your Arabic choices would force an editor to change the artwork?
Practice routine based on the discussion in Lathey (2016, Ch. 3); use any picture book you have at home or one your instructor shows in class.

Four strategies for a sound-word
1 · Keep
Leave the source sound as printed. Most likely for sound-letters that are part of the artwork (Kaindl, cited in Lathey, 2016), but a risk for a young reader of Arabic.
2 · Swap
Replace it with the customary Arabic sound, as in tick-tock → تِك تَاك.
3 · Re-voice
Invent a sound the child can voice and that fits the action (for example TUNK! → طاخ!), spoken aloud first.
4 · Re-letter
If the sound is drawn into the picture, ask whether the artwork can be re-lettered; if not, you may have to keep it.
Strategy labels are a teaching summary of the options discussed in Lathey (2016, Ch. 3); Zitawi’s Disney findings are cited there.

Inscriptions are words drawn inside the picture: shop names, signs, banners, letters, newspapers, numbers. In a co-production the translated text is added to finished pictures, so changing this lettering may be impossible. Decide keep, gloss, or translate, and let the story decide.
Decision guide · Keep, gloss or translate?
Keep
Shop names and environmental print show the child where the story is set. Keeping them preserves the sense of difference that is part of the pleasure of translations.
Translate
When a poster, banner, letter or news story carries information the child needs to follow the plot, the words must be translated.
Gloss
If the lettering cannot be changed but its meaning matters, carry the meaning in the running text (without over-explaining the picture).
Examples discussed in Lathey (2016, Ch. 3): the German edition of Voices in the Park, Hergé’s L’Île Noire, and the Beagle Boys’ numbers in Arabic Disney (Zitawi).
Mini-project · Sign audit

Photograph or sketch five signs from your own street (in Arabic, English, or both). For each, write: (1) the sign; (2) what it tells a reader about the place; (3) if it appeared inside a picture book illustration for children abroad, would you keep, gloss or translate it, and why? Bring your audit to class.

  • Did at least one sign carry plot-vital information?
  • Did at least one sign mainly give a sense of place?
  • Did you notice any sign whose lettering style carries mood (hand-lettered, neon, formal)?
