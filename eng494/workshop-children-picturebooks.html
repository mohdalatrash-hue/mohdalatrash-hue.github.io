📖 What this lab is for

This is the capstone of Unit 2. Every task asks the same two questions: What is the problem in the story? and Which idea in the readings explains it? Answers that name a concept and give a page reference (for example, Lathey, 2016, p. 59) earn the marks. Read the stories in your Blackboard copies; page numbers below refer to those PDFs. Nothing you type here is sent anywhere: it stays in your browser, so copy your work into your Word file.

4

Debate cards

15 min
How to run it: pick two cards (or the cards your instructor assigns). Prepare for two minutes, speak for one minute, then open the notes. A strong answer cites two readings.
Card 1 of 6

Picture book or comic?

Use: Ch. 3 pp. 58, 61–62

Lathey says picture books and comic strips share translation strategies (p. 61). Using Wild Things and Caterpillar against Calvin and Spider-Man, identify two constraints they share and two that differ.

3:00
After your talk: what to listen for
Shared: matching text to image, onomatopoeia, reading aloud, the book as artefact. Different: speech-bubble fit and dialogue dominance in comics (pp. 61–63); minimal text, read-aloud performance and the page-turn in picturebooks (pp. 58–59). Strong answers cite Kaindl's three elements (p. 62).
Card 2 of 6

Who is the translator in the production chain?

Use: Ch. 3 pp. 55, 58, 66–68

Lathey's discussion points ask whether the translator is 'a straightforward provider of text or a contributor to an editorial team' (p. 68) and cite Oittinen's call for specialised training (p. 55). Apply this to the Peppa Pig tie-in and to a digital Spider-Man re-lettering workflow.

3:00
After your talk: what to listen for
Co-production: text inserted last into fixed art (p. 58). Lettering by graphics programme (Zanettin, pp. 66–67). Translator should negotiate fit, lettering and layout; training in art appreciation (Oittinen).
Card 3 of 6

Retain or replace the sound?

Use: Ch. 3 pp. 63–64; Ch. 1 pp. 31–32; Ch. 4 p. 74

Set a class policy for sound words in an Arabic edition of Calvin and Hobbes. Consider Kaindl's and Zitawi's findings and the child still learning to read. Compare with Hebrew vocalisation and Japanese hiragana as reading aids (Lathey, pp. 31–32, 74). What aids exist in Arabic children's books?

3:00
After your talk: what to listen for
Zitawi: nearly always replaced; the child still learning Arabic (p. 64). Lathey's analogies: vocalisation in Hebrew (p. 74) and hiragana/katakana (pp. 31–32). Students may add Arabic tashkeel (full vowelling) as an Arabic equivalent: legibility is part of the decision.
Card 4 of 6

Which Arabic for Grandpa Pig?

Use: Ch. 4 pp. 73, 83–84, 90

Choose a variety for the Arabic Peppa Pig book and defend it under three pressures: pan-Arab distribution, a parent reading aloud, and the child's own speech.

3:00
After your talk: what to listen for
MSA by default (p. 73); Sharafeddine's hybrid; avoid a localised dialect that 'locates' the characters (pp. 83–84); read aloud and test on children (p. 90). Look for a decision, not a list.
Card 5 of 6

Mirror the panels or reorder them?

Use: Ch. 3 pp. 67–68

Your course copy says that 'Arabic is read from left to right' (p. 67). Spot the slip, then explain what Zitawi's two methods (printing panels in reverse; re-ordering them) mean for Calvin's strips and Spider-Man's pages: handedness, lettering in the artwork, sound words, balloon order.

3:00
After your talk: what to listen for
Arabic is read right to left. Mirroring reverses handedness and any lettering in the art (and signs, p. 65); re-ordering keeps the art but risks balloon-order confusion (pp. 67–68). Cultural practices tied to the right hand are the example Lathey gives.
Card 6 of 6

Mediation or censorship?

Use: Ch. 1 pp. 25–27, 29; Ch. 3 pp. 59, 61

A salami in the Saturday list, a school 'paddling' in Calvin, a romance scene and a gambling parlour in Spider-Man: where does acceptable mediation end and censorship begin? Argue with Lindgren's dung heap (p. 26) and with the rule against over-explaining (p. 29).

3:00
After your talk: what to listen for
Lathey: censorship demands editorial debate (p. 27 summary); sanitising can remove the child's delight (p. 26); over-explanation is a pitfall (p. 29); but cultural sensitivities are real (pp. 26–27). Look for a process (identify the function of the item, consult the editor, choose the lightest change).
