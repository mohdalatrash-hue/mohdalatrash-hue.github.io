Task 1A · kill the poem, then save it
Charmed magic casements, opening on the foam Of perilous seas, in faery lands forlorn.— John Keats, “Ode to a Nightingale” (1819) · public domain · the lines Wood uses on p. 97

Step 1. Do what Wood’s “trot” does: translate the two lines into Arabic word for word, choosing a dictionary meaning for each word.

Step 2. Now recreate the lines as Arabic poetry: keep the magic, not the dictionary.

What Wood’s point predicts

Your Step 1 version is probably “correct” and dead: every word is right, but nothing enchants. That is exactly Wood’s claim on pp. 97–98: with absolute fidelity “the poetry is dead”; it must be recreated.

One possible pair

حرفيًّا: «نوافذُ سحريةٌ مسحورةٌ، تنفتحُ على زَبَدِ بحارٍ خطِرةٍ، في أراضي الجِنِّيّاتِ البائسة.»

إعادة خلق: «نوافذُ مسحورةٌ تُطِلُّ على زَبَدِ البحارِ المُهلِكة، في دنيا الجِنِّ الحزينةِ النائية.»

Check yourself

  • Did Step 2 change words in order to save the music?
  • Did you keep the key images (windows, foam, danger, fairyland, loneliness)?
  • Read both aloud: which one would a reader remember?
Task 1B · who decides what a “poem” is? (p. 98)

Landers says to ask first what the target audience regards as a poem. For English readers he names iambic pentameter, blank verse and, today, unrhymed metrical verse. Write 2–3 sentences: what forms would an Arabic reader accept as a poem (for example عمودي with وزن and قافية, شعر التفعيلة, قصيدة النثر), and which would you choose for a translated English love poem? Why?

  • Landers’ test: the final product must stand as a poem in its own right in the target language.
  • His warning (Iglesias): the translator is not more important than the poem, so the form must serve the original, not show off.
  • A good answer links the choice to the reader and to the source poem’s own form.
Read first: pp. 98–99, “To rhyme or not to rhyme?”: Landers on English as rhyme-poor, Shapiro’s reply, Quevedo’s sonnet, and “verse is not rhyme”.
Task 2A · the debate

Landers says English is rhyme-poor; Shapiro says its huge vocabulary more than compensates. Choose a side, then write three sentences using evidence from the reading (an example word pair, a language comparison).

Landers (p. 98)

Few rhymes for key words (love: four rhymes, one useless); Romance languages rhyme easily (padre/madre, amare/odiare, âme/dame, sorte/morte); many verbs rhyme across their forms in Romance.

Shapiro (pp. 98–99)

English has a far larger lexicon; plural nouns rhyme with verb forms (apples/dapples, facts/acts); Italian and Portuguese, and even more Spanish, are weaker here; the difficulty is part of the fun.

Task 2B · now turn it around: Arabic

Landers writes about translating into English. Our direction is into Arabic. Is Arabic closer to “rhyme-poor” English or to the “rhyme-rich” Romance languages? Give two Arabic word pairs that rhyme easily because of shared patterns or endings.

Arabic behaves more like Landers’ Romance languages: shared morphological patterns and endings make rhyme easy, e.g. قلوب / دروب, أحلام / أيّام, ساهرين / حالمين. So for us the bigger danger is not finding a rhyme but Quevedo’s problem: letting the rhyme dictate the meaning.

Task 3A · the read-aloud test
I wandered lonely as a cloud That floats on high o’er vales and hills,— William Wordsworth (1807) · public domain

Read both Arabic versions aloud, twice each. Which one would Landers prefer, and why?

أ) «تجوّلتُ وحيدًا مثلَ غيمةٍ تطفو في الأعالي فوقَ الوديانِ والتلال»
ب) «هِمتُ وحيدًا كغيمةٍ / تطفو عاليًا فوقَ تلٍّ ووادِ»

Version B is shorter and has a beat: هِمتُ carries “I wandered” in one word, and the line breaks give it breath. Version A is accurate but reads like prose. Landers (p. 100): every translation must be read aloud, and a translation that sounds bad is, almost certainly, bad. Notice that B also pays a price: “vales and hills” has been reduced to one hill and one valley to keep the rhythm.

Task 3B · the double meaning (p. 100)

In Buarque’s line, viram can mean “they saw (Jesus)” or “they become (Jesus)”. Arabic cannot hold both in one verb. What are your options? Choose the one you would take and justify it.

Landers asked the author. Buarque was surprised and pleased: he had not consciously intended the double meaning. Landers’ lesson: there is no better source than the author, but in poetry even the author may not know exactly what an allusion “means”. Any of the options above can be defended; what matters is that you notice the ambiguity and decide deliberately.

Task 4A · Arabic gains a syllable

Landers notes that Spanish piensa (“he thinks”) costs English an extra syllable for the pronoun. Arabic is like Spanish here. Translate these into Arabic and count how many words you need compared with English.

I wandered · She sings · They waited

هِمتُ · تُغنّي · انتظَروا

Each two-word English phrase becomes one Arabic word. Translating into Arabic often gives the translator room to spare, which is a reason not to pad.

Task 5B · save the funny rhyme for last

This Arabic couplet puts the joke in the wrong place. Rewrite it so the funny word lands at the very end.

«الكبسةُ أكلتُها كلَّها، أنا البطلُ! — قالَ الطبيبُ: خفِّفْ قليلًا يا رجلُ»

«قالَ الطبيبُ: خفِّفِ الأكلَ يا رجلْ / فخفّفتُ… وأكلتُ الكبسةَ بالعسلْ!»

The surprise (بالعسل) now arrives last, on the rhyme: Landers’ rule 3.

Task 5C · the “unrhymable” challenge

Landers shows that, with ingenuity, even orange and month can be rhymed when the goal is humour. Choose one Arabic word that seems hard to rhyme (for example برتقالة, ثلاجة, واجب منزلي) and write a funny two-line rhyme that ends on it.
