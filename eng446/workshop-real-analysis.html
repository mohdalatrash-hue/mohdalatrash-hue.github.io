How Real Articles Write Data Analysis

Three real published studies on AI in English education. Every one of them used a pre-test/post-test and a survey — exactly your design. Download them, then see how each turns numbers into a section.

📥 All three are free and legal to download

These are open-access articles published under a Creative Commons Attribution (CC BY) licence — you may download, keep and quote them, provided you cite them properly. Nothing here is behind a paywall and nothing needs a library login. Download all three before you start the workshop; every task below sends you back into the PDFs.

Each card gives you the citation in APA 7, what the study actually measured, and a direct download. Read the Results / Data Analysis section of each one — that is the part you are copying the craft of, not the topic.

1
Enhancing academic writing skills and motivation: assessing the efficacy of ChatGPT in AI-assisted language learning for EFL students
Song, C., & Song, Y. (2023). Frontiers in Psychology, 14, 1260843.
North Minzu University & Central South University, China · Published 15 December 2023
Open access · CC BY 4.0 Pre/post test Motivation survey Interviews Uses ANCOVA
Design
12-week quasi-experiment · 50 Chinese EFL students · experimental group used ChatGPT, control group had teacher-led instruction
Test instrument
IELTS academic writing tasks 1 & 2, scored for content, organisation and language use
Survey instrument
Writing Motivation Scale — 7 items, 5-point Likert, Cronbach's α = 0.86
Also collected
Semi-structured interviews with 9 students from the experimental group
Analysis used
Descriptive statistics → independent-samples t-tests → a series of one-way ANCOVAs using the pre-test as covariate, with partial η² effect sizes
⬇ Download the PDF Read online ↗ DOI ↗
Why this one is on the list: it is the closest to a textbook example. It reports the descriptive table first, then the inferential test, then the effect size — and it uses the pre-test as a covariate rather than just comparing gains. If your groups were not equal at the start, this is the model to follow.
2
Evaluating the Effectiveness of Chatbot-Assisted Learning in Enhancing English Conversational Skills Among Secondary School Students
Alenezi, A., & Alenezi, A. (2025). Education Sciences, 15(9), 1136.
Northern Border University, Arar & Jouf University, Al-Jawf — Saudi Arabia · Published 1 September 2025
Open access · CC BY 4.0 Pre/post test Attitude questionnaire Observation rubric Uses paired & independent t-tests
Design
3-week intervention · 30 secondary students aged 15–17 in the Northern Borders Region · 15 experimental, 15 control
Test instrument
Structured conversation tasks marked on a 5-point rubric out of 20 — fluency, vocabulary, grammar, pronunciation, contextual relevance
Survey instrument
10-item student attitude questionnaire, 5-point Likert — enjoyment, motivation, confidence, perceived gains, satisfaction
Also collected
Teacher observation rubric (two independent raters) and automatic chatbot usage logs
Analysis used
Descriptive statistics → paired t-tests within each group → independent t-tests between groups → Pearson correlation between usage time and improvement
⬇ Download the PDF Read online ↗ DOI ↗
Why this one is on the list: it is Saudi, it is small (30 students — about the size of your own samples), and it is the most imitable. It also does something the other two do not: it correlates how much students used the tool with how much they improved. If you logged any usage data, this is your model.
3
AI-generated corpus learning and EFL learners' learning of grammatical structures, lexical bundles, and willingness to write
Lu, C. (2025). PLOS ONE, 20(7), e0321544.
Baotou Teachers' College, China · Published 11 July 2025
Open access · CC BY 4.0 Pre/post + delayed test Willingness scale Uses mixed ANOVA
Design
14 sessions · 80 EFL students at Baotou Teachers' College · 40 experimental (AI-generated corpus), 40 control (textbook)
Test instrument
Two 30-item multiple-choice tests — grammatical structures and lexical bundles (KR-21 = 0.79 pre, 0.81 post)
Survey instrument
Willingness to Write Scale — 8 items adapted from MacIntyre et al., Cronbach's α = 0.83
Distinctive move
Added a delayed post-test (follow-up) to test whether the gain lasted
Analysis used
Mixed ANOVA — time (pre/post/follow-up) × group — reporting main effects, the interaction effect, and η²
⬇ Download the PDF Read online ↗ DOI ↗
Why this one is on the list: three time points instead of two. The interaction effect is the finding that matters here — it is what tells you the two groups changed differently over time, which is the actual claim an intervention study wants to make.
⚠️ A caution before you imitate anything. Published does not mean flawless. All three of these studies have small samples, and one of them reports no assumption checks at all before running its ANOVA. Part of your job in Tab 3 is to notice what each one does not report. Read like a researcher, not like a fan.

Strip away the topic and every good Data Analysis section does the same six things in the same order. Here is the pattern, illustrated with Article 1 because it follows it most cleanly.

The six moves
1
Say what you did to the data before you tested anything
Which software, how scores were computed, how raters were reconciled, how incomplete responses were handled. One short paragraph. This is the move students skip most often.
2
Report reliability
Cronbach's α for a Likert survey; KR-21 or KR-20 for a right/wrong test; inter-rater agreement if humans marked anything. Article 1 reports α = 0.86 for its motivation scale, Article 3 reports α = 0.83 and KR-21 = 0.79/0.81. A survey with no reported reliability is a survey a reader cannot trust.
3
Show descriptive statistics first — in a table
Means and standard deviations for every group at every time point. The reader must be able to see the raw picture before you tell them what it means. Never open with a p-value.
4
State the test and why that test
"Because the two groups differed slightly at pre-test, a one-way ANCOVA was run with the pre-test as covariate." The reason is as important as the test.
5
Report the result in the standard format
Statistic, degrees of freedom, value, p, and effect size — in that order, e.g. F(1, 47) = 19.01, p < .001, partial η² = 0.27.
6
Say what it means in one plain sentence
A number is not a finding until you say what it shows. "The experimental group outperformed the control group on overall writing after controlling for pre-test scores."
1
Move 3 in practice — Article 1's descriptive table
Song & Song (2023), overall writing score. These are the article's own reported figures.
GroupPre-test M (SD)Post-test M (SD)Change
Experimental (ChatGPT)39.26 (12.03)59.12 (14.23)+19.86
Control (teacher-led)37.31 (17.26)45.18 (15.62)+7.87

Both groups improved. That is why a control group exists — without it, the +19.86 looks like proof and is not.

Then moves 4–6, in the article's own logic

The two groups were not identical at pre-test (39.26 vs 37.31), so a simple comparison of post-test scores would be unfair to the control group. The article therefore runs a one-way ANCOVA with the pre-test score as the covariate — statistically levelling the starting line — and reports:

F(1, 47) = 19.01, p < .001, partial η² = 0.27

Read left to right: an F test with 1 and 47 degrees of freedom returned 19.01; the probability of seeing a difference this large by chance is under one in a thousand; and the grouping accounts for about 27% of the variance in writing scores — a large effect. The same three-part report appears again for motivation: F(1, 47) = 29.62, p < .001, partial η² = 0.38.

The mark students lose most often. Reporting p and stopping. p tells you the difference is probably not luck; it says nothing about whether the difference is big enough to matter. That is what the effect size is for. With 30 participants you can have a real effect and a p above .05 — and with 3,000 you can have a trivial effect and p < .001.

All three studies ran the same basic design — two groups, a test before and after, plus a survey. All three chose a different statistical route. Which one you copy depends on what your own data looks like.

Which route fits your project?
A
Your two groups started roughly equal → independent t-test on post-test scores
Simplest defensible choice. Article 2's route. Report the paired t-test within each group as well, so the reader sees who improved and who did not.
B
Your groups started unequal → ANCOVA with pre-test as covariate
Article 1's route. This is the honest answer to "but the experimental group was already better."
C
You measured three or more times → mixed ANOVA
Article 3's route. The interaction is your headline, not the main effects.
D
One group only, no control → paired t-test, and be honest in your limitations
Perfectly publishable at your level, but you cannot claim the intervention caused the gain. Say so explicitly rather than hoping nobody notices.
Read critically — what these articles do not do

Open each PDF and check for yourself whether the following appear. In a strong Results section they should. Where they are missing, that is a limitation the authors should have named:

  • Assumption checks — normality, Levene's test for equal variances, sphericity for repeated measures. Check Article 3 particularly carefully here.
  • Effect sizes on every test, not just the headline one.
  • Exact p values rather than only "p < .001" or "p = 0.00" — a p can never truly equal zero.
  • Group sizes stated in the results, not only in the methods.
  • A limitations paragraph that admits what the small sample prevents them from claiming.
This is a real skill, not a fault-finding game. Every study is a set of compromises. The mark of a research-methods student is being able to say precisely which compromise was made and what it costs the conclusion.

Four tasks. Do them with the PDFs open beside you. Your answers save on this device.

1Find the six moves
Open Article 1 (Song & Song) and go to the Results section. Locate each of the six moves from Tab 2 and write down the page or paragraph where it happens. Mark any move that is missing.
What you should have found. Article 1 is strong on moves 2, 3, 5 and 6 and thinner on move 1.
  • Move 2 is in the instruments section rather than the results — Cronbach's α = 0.86 for the Writing Motivation Scale. Reliability reported in Methods is perfectly acceptable; reliability reported nowhere is not.
  • Move 3 is Table 2: pre- and post-test means and SDs for both groups across overall writing, content, organisation, language use and motivation.
  • Move 4 is explicit — the ANCOVA is chosen so the pre-test can be controlled as a covariate.
  • Move 5 appears in Tables 3–7, each with F, df, p and partial η².
  • Move 6 follows each table in prose.
  • Move 1 is the weak one — there is little detail on how the IELTS tasks were marked and reconciled between raters. That is the gap you should not imitate.
2Match the situation to the test
Choose the analysis each situation calls for.
3Read a real table and write the sentence
These are Article 2's actual reported figures for the conversation task, scored out of 20. Write the two or three sentences that would go underneath this table in a Results section. Use the format from Tab 2: describe, then test, then meaning.
GroupPre-test M (SD)Post-test M (SD)Reported test
Experimental (chatbot), n = 1510.40 (1.56)15.64 (2.11)t(14) = 12.78, p < .001
Control, n = 1510.73 (1.41)11.81 (1.65)p = .12

Alenezi & Alenezi (2025). The study also reports a Pearson r = 0.61 between minutes of chatbot use and score improvement.

A model paragraph. "Both groups began at a comparable level (experimental M = 10.40, SD = 1.56; control M = 10.73, SD = 1.41). After the three-week intervention the experimental group rose to M = 15.64 (SD = 2.11), a gain that a paired-samples t-test confirmed as statistically significant, t(14) = 12.78, p < .001. The control group's smaller rise to M = 11.81 (SD = 1.65) was not significant (p = .12). A Pearson correlation further indicated a moderate-to-strong positive relationship between time spent using the chatbot and improvement in score, r = .61, suggesting that the gain was related to how much the tool was actually used."
  • Notice it opens by establishing the groups were comparable at baseline — that single clause is what licenses everything after it.
  • Notice the control group's non-significant result is reported, not hidden. Reporting what did not work is not a weakness; concealing it is misconduct.
  • Notice the correlation is described as related, not as caused by.
4Draft your own group's Data Analysis section
Now write the real thing for your project, using whichever of the three articles matches your design. Answer these in order, in continuous prose:
  1. How was your data prepared — scoring, entry, software, missing responses?
  2. What is the reliability of your instrument, and how did you obtain it?
  3. What are your descriptive statistics? (Put them in a table.)
  4. Which test did you run, and why that one?
  5. What is the full result — statistic, df, value, p, effect size?
  6. What does it mean, in one sentence a non-statistician would follow?
Mark your own draft against this list.
  • Have you put a table of means and SDs before any test result?
  • Did you name the test and justify it in the same sentence?
  • Is every statistic reported in full — not just p?
  • Have you given an effect size?
  • Have you avoided the word "prove" entirely?
  • Have you reported anything that did not reach significance?
  • Have you kept interpretation out of the results and saved it for the discussion?
  • Are all three articles you consulted in your APA reference list?
