Methodology + Discussion Sections

Due date: Saturday, 1 August 2026, 11:59 p.m.

You already collected your survey and interview data.

Now you must write and submit TWO sections of your paper:

  1. Methodology (or Method)
  2. Discussion

Use the three model articles we studied in class as guides.

📎 Research-Qualitative-Sample.pdf and 2 more files — attached to the assignment on Blackboard.

Worked examples: three real published studies, and a walkthrough of how each one writes its Data Analysis section — open the walkthrough →

Part A – Methodology / Method section

Submit one section titled Methodology (or Method).

Inside it, use these five sub-headings, in this order:

A1
Design
  • State your research design (e.g., quantitative survey, qualitative interviews, mixed methods).
  • 2–3 sentences only.
A2
Participants
  • Who took part (number, level/year, major, gender if relevant, setting).
  • How they were selected (sampling) and any inclusion criteria.
A3
Instruments
  • Survey: number of items, type of scale, what it measures.
  • Interviews: number of questions, main focus.
  • Mention if you adapted any instrument from previous studies (with APA citation).
A4
Data Collection
  • When, where, and how you collected the survey data.
  • When, where, and how you conducted the interviews (mode, language, duration, recording, consent).
A5
Data Analysis
  • Quantitative: which statistics you used (e.g., descriptive statistics, t-tests, etc.) and which software (if any).
  • Qualitative: how you coded and analysed interviews (e.g., thematic analysis; basic steps).

Part B – Discussion section

After your Methodology, write a Discussion section that interprets your findings.

Use this simple structure:

B1
Overview of main findings
  • One short paragraph summarising your key results (no tables, just sentences).
B2
Comparison with previous studies
  • Use at least 5 different scholarly sources (journal articles, books, or chapters).
  • Show how your findings: Agree with, Disagree with, or Add something new to previous studies.
  • Use APA in-text citations for each source.
B3
Possible explanations
  • Offer 1–2 short paragraphs explaining why you think you found these results (context, participants, instruments, etc.).
B4
Implications and/or limitations
Briefly mention:
  • What teachers, students, or policymakers can learn from your findings (implications).
  • One or two limitations (e.g., small sample, one institution, self-report data).

APA format (for both sections)

For Methodology and Discussion:

  • Follow APA 7 as discussed in class:
  • Times New Roman, 12-pt, double spacing, 1-inch margins.
  • Correct APA-style headings and sub-headings.
  • Use APA in-text citations whenever you refer to any source (instrument, theory, previous study).
  • Your full reference list will be included at the end of the complete paper, but all citations in these two sections must already follow APA style.

Academic integrity – AI use is banned

⚠️ For this assignment, all text must be written by you.

  • AI-generated text is not allowed. This includes tools such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot, etc.
  • You may use basic spelling/grammar checkers (e.g., Word spell-check), but do not use any AI tool to generate or rewrite sentences or paragraphs.
  • If any part of your Methodology or Discussion is found to be AI-generated, the grade for this assignment will be zero (0) and it may be reported as an academic integrity violation.

Quick checklist before you submit

  • ✓I included both sections: Methodology/Method and Discussion.
  • ✓My Methodology has Design, Participants, Instruments, Data Collection, and Data Analysis.
  • ✓My Discussion summarises findings, compares them with at least 5 sources, and mentions explanations and implications/limitations.
  • ✓I followed APA 7 for headings, in-text citations, and formatting.
  • ✓I did not use AI to generate or rewrite any part of the text.
In-class discussion — bring an answer to each

Five questions on the brief above. Quote the clause number when you answer. Your notes save on this device.

1What exactly does A5 ask you to report?
Read A5 · Data Analysis. It asks for two different things — one for the survey, one for the interviews. In your own words, what is the minimum that would satisfy each half? What would not satisfy it?
The quantitative half wants which statistics and which software — "I used descriptive statistics and independent-samples t-tests in SPSS" satisfies it; "I analysed the data" does not. The qualitative half wants the procedure, not the result — how you moved from transcripts to codes to themes. Naming the approach (thematic analysis) and giving the basic steps satisfies it; "I read the interviews and found themes" does not, because a reader cannot tell what you actually did.
2B2 says "agree, disagree, or add something new" — which is hardest?
Most students write only agree. Why is that the weakest of the three? What does a Discussion gain when a group is willing to say their finding disagrees with a published study?
Agreement is safe and often uninformative — if your finding only ever confirms everyone, you have not told the field anything it did not have. Disagreement is where a small study earns its place, provided you then do B3 and explain why: a different context, different learners, a different instrument, a smaller sample. A disagreement you cannot explain is a problem; a disagreement you can explain is a contribution.
3Why does B4 ask for limitations at all?
It can feel like arguing against your own work. What does a marker — or a journal reviewer — actually conclude about a paper that names its limitations clearly, versus one that names none?
Naming limitations does not weaken a study; it shows you understand what your design can and cannot support. A paper with no limitations reads either as naive or as concealing something, and a reviewer will supply the limitations for you — less generously. The three examples in B4 (small sample, one institution, self-report data) apply to almost every project in this class. The skill is saying what each one costs your conclusion: a single institution means you cannot generalise beyond it, so do not.
4Where is the line on the AI rule?
The brief bans AI-generated text but allows a spell-checker. Decide as a group which side of the line each of these falls on: (a) asking an AI to explain what a t-test is; (b) asking it to rewrite your paragraph "more academically"; (c) using it to find articles; (d) translating your own Arabic sentence into English with an AI tool.
The brief's test is whether the text you submit was generated or rewritten by a tool.
  • (a) Learning — you then write your own sentence. Not banned.
  • (b) Rewriting your paragraph — this is exactly what the brief prohibits, even though the ideas were yours.
  • (c) Finding articles — a search aid; you still read and cite them yourself.
  • (d) Translating — the submitted English sentence was produced by a tool, so treat it as banned here and write it yourself.
If you are unsure about a case, ask before you submit rather than after.
5Audit your own draft against the brief
Go through A1–A5 and B1–B4 and mark each one done, thin, or missing in your group's draft. Write the two you are weakest on and what you will add.
Across previous cohorts the two thinnest are almost always A5 and B3. A5 because groups describe their results instead of their procedure; B3 because explaining why a result happened requires a claim, and claims feel riskier than description. Both are also where the easiest marks are, because both simply require you to write down what you actually did and what you actually think.
